# Security Incident Response and Impact Analysis Procedure

**Document ID:** SEC-PRO-014
**Version:** 4.2
**Owner:** AI Security Lead (security incidents); Data Protection Officer (personal-data impact
determinations); General Counsel (notification decisions); the Incident Commander on duty owns the
individual incident
**Approved by:** CEO 2026-02-19; reviewed by the Audit Committee 2026-02-19
**Next scheduled review:** 2026-12-31 (Annually, and after every Sev-1, every near miss with a control failure, and)
every exercise — whichever comes first
**Scope:** All security events affecting the compliance-evaluation service in AWS account 471885797689
(us-west-2), the corporate environment, and the seven outsourced providers whose services touch entity or
customer data. Applies to both legal entities and all five geographies.

## 1. Response procedures — the defined path from event to closure

**The entity has procedures in place for responding to security incidents.** The procedure is a single
documented path with named roles, defined severities and mandatory steps; the runbooks live in the
repository next to the systems they cover, so they are current at the point of use rather than in a binder.

**Roles.** Six personnel are certified **Incident Commanders** (raised from two after the 2025 competence
review found incident command thin beyond two people — four were trained and two have since led a live
incident). Twelve personnel are **on-call certified**, renewed annually with quarterly game days. The IC
owns the incident and has authority to take production action, engage providers, and declare severity
without further approval. Supporting roles: Technical Lead, Communications Lead, Scribe, and — engaged by
name rather than by hoping someone thinks of it — the **DPO for any personal-data possibility** and the
**General Counsel for any notification question**. The IC is explicitly *not* the person who found the
event, so that discovery and response judgment are separated.

**Severity criteria** (declared on the information available; **a severity is raised readily and lowered
only with the IC's recorded reasoning**, because under-declaration is the failure mode this procedure is
designed against):

| Sev | Criteria | Response commitments |
|---|---|---|
| **Sev-1** | Confirmed or credible unauthorized access to customer evidence, assessment results or personal data; a wrong verdict issued to a customer; loss of tenant isolation; service unavailable beyond the availability tolerance; ransomware or destructive action; credential compromise with production reach | Page acknowledged **within 15 minutes**; IC assigned immediately; status updates every 30 minutes; executive notification within 1 hour; **customer notification within 24 hours of confirmation**; post-incident review **mandatory** |
| Sev-2 | Suspected unauthorized access without confirmation; a security control confirmed absent or bypassed on a production system; loss or theft of a device; a provider incident with potential entity impact; degraded service within tolerance | Acknowledged within 30 minutes; updates every 2 hours; post-incident review mandatory where a control failed |
| Sev-3 | A security event requiring investigation with no immediate indication of compromise; a policy violation with data-access implications | Next business day; review at the monthly control meeting |
| **Near miss** | An event that did not cause impact but would have if one further control had failed | **Post-incident review mandatory** — treated as a Sev-2 for learning purposes, because a near miss is free information about a real weakness |

**Mandatory phases**, each with a defined exit condition rather than a feeling of completion:

1. **Detect and report** — from automated alerting (§3), a person, a customer, a researcher, or a provider.
   A single phone number is published for device loss and urgent reports; the email address it replaced was
   removed after a post-incident review found it added minutes to a device-loss report.
2. **Triage and declare** — the on-call engineer triages against the severity criteria within the
   acknowledgement window and assigns an IC. **Declaration is not gated on certainty**; an incident may be
   declared and stood down.
3. **Contain** — isolate, revoke credentials, block network paths, suspend an affected assessment class.
   Containment actions are pre-authorized in the runbook so the IC does not need to seek approval mid-event;
   the IC records what was done and why.
4. **Analyze and determine impact** — §6, the substantive analysis phase.
5. **Eradicate and recover** — remove the cause, restore from a tested path (the quarterly restore test is
   what makes this credible), and verify.
6. **Notify** — internal, customer, regulatory and provider notifications per §7's timers.
7. **Post-incident review** — mandatory for Sev-1, Sev-2 with a control failure, and every near miss.
   The review must state whether the risk register was wrong, and produce dated remediation with owners.
8. **Close** — closure requires the remediation to be verified by **someone other than the owner** and the
   deficiency register updated; an incident cannot be closed while its remediation is open.

**Interfaces:** the procedure interlocks with the change procedure (an emergency change made during an
incident still carries a blocking post-hoc review before the next deploy), the deficiency procedure
(MON-PRO-011 — incident-derived findings enter the same register with the same 2-business-day capture rule),
the privacy breach procedure (§7), the continuity plan, and each provider's incident-notification obligation
in its requirements annex.

## 2. Periodic evaluation of the effectiveness of the response procedures

**The entity periodically evaluates the effectiveness of its incident response policies and procedures.**
Evaluation is deliberately not limited to reviewing incidents that happened, because a period with few
incidents would otherwise produce no evidence.

| Evaluation activity | Cadence | What is tested | Result this period |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quarterly game day** — an unannounced simulated incident against a real (non-production) environment | Quarterly | Detection, paging, IC decision-making, containment runbook accuracy, communication discipline | 4 held. **2026-Q1 exposed a stale runbook** — a containment step referenced a network construct that had been replaced; the runbook was corrected and a repository check added so runbooks referencing renamed resources fail the build |
| **Annual full-scale exercise** including executive and legal participation, and a scenario requiring a notification decision | Annually | End-to-end incl. notification decision-making under time pressure, and the DPO's engagement | Held 2025-11. **Finding: the notification decision took 31 hours in the exercise against a 24-hour commitment**, because the legal-review step had no pre-drafted material. Remediation: pre-approved notification templates per scenario class and a standing 2-hour legal review slot on declaration of Sev-1. Re-tested in the 2026-Q1 game day at 6 hours |
| **Tabletop for provider-originated incidents** | Annually | Whether the entity can respond when the failing component is not its own | Held 2026-01; produced the change requiring each provider's annex to name a notification contact and a maximum notification time |
| **Purple-team detection validation** | Semi-annually | Whether the detections that response depends on actually fire | Added this period specifically because **R-039 (detection failure) is invisible without deliberate testing**; the first run confirmed 9 of 11 expected detections fired and drove two alerting changes |
| **Metrics review** — MTTD, MTTA, MTTR, severity distribution, reopen rate, and **the proportion of incidents found by the entity versus reported by others** | Monthly at the control review; quarterly to the Audit Committee | Whether response is getting faster and whether the entity is finding its own problems | MTTD improved; **the entity was told about 2 of 6 events this period by outside parties (a researcher and a customer), which is recorded as an unfavourable detection indicator rather than as successful reporting channels** |
| **Post-incident review quality check** | Quarterly sample by the Compliance Manager | Whether reviews reach root cause and produce verified remediation, or stop at a proximate cause | 1 review was returned as insufficient — it named an individual's mistake as the root cause without asking why the system allowed it |
| **Independent assessment** | Annually by internal audit; externally within the penetration test scope | Procedure design against the criteria; sample of incidents against the procedure | Internal audit found severity was under-declared on one Sev-3 that met Sev-2 criteria; correction is the recorded rule that severity is raised readily and lowered only with reasoning |
| **Coverage review against the risk register** | Annually and on register change | Whether every Critical/High risk with an incident pathway has a runbook | Runbooks added for prompt-injection response and for a provider sub-processor breach |

**Evaluation findings are recorded as deficiencies and tracked to closure like any other** — the 31-hour
notification decision, the stale runbook, the under-declared severity and the insufficient review are all
in the register with owners and dates. An evaluation programme that produced no findings would be evidence
that the evaluation was not real.

## 3. Communicating detected security events to those responsible for the security program

**Detected security events are communicated to the individuals responsible for the management of the
security program.** Communication is routed by automation to named roles, not to a shared inbox that
depends on someone reading it.

| Source of the detected event | Routed to | Mechanism | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security monitoring and alerting (entity-operated) | On-call engineer; **AI Security Lead** for high-severity classes | Paging system, with the alert naming the runbook | Immediate; page acknowledged within 15 min for the Sev-1 classes |
| **Managed SIEM provider** (depth of detection) | On-call engineer and the AI Security Lead simultaneously | Provider's alert integration into the entity's paging system — **not** a provider portal someone must check | Immediate, with the provider's annex naming a maximum notification time |
| Configuration compliance and drift detection | Resource owner **and** the AI Security Lead | Automatic ticket plus alert | Immediate for security-relevant classes |
| Break-glass / emergency access use | **People outside the identity function** — the AI Security Lead and the Compliance Manager — by design, since the identity administrators are the ones using it (the R-041 compensating control) | Alert on every use | Immediate |
| Anomalous verdict shift, bulk read, or DLP egress | AI Security Lead and the accountable data owner | Alert | Immediate |
| Log-configuration change (a tampering indicator) | AI Security Lead and Compliance Manager | Alert | Immediate |
| A person — any employee, contractor or vendor individual | On-call via the published number, and to the AI Security Lead | Published single number for urgent reports; a form for non-urgent; **the ethics channel including anonymous reporting is a valid route and is monitored** | Immediate on suspicion, and the standing instruction is to report on suspicion rather than after self-investigating |
| **Suspected personal-data involvement** | **The DPO, immediately on suspicion, in parallel with security** — never sequentially after security concludes | Page to the DPO, who acknowledges within 1 hour | Immediate |
| A customer (via support, the named contact, or their own monitoring) | Customer Operations, who must raise a security event record within 1 hour; and to the AI Security Lead | Support workflow with a mandatory security-flag question | Within 1 hour of receipt |
| **An external security researcher** | AI Security Lead directly, via the published disclosure address with a stated response commitment | Published vulnerability disclosure route | Acknowledged within 1 business day |
| A provider or sub-processor | Named entity-side engagement owner and the AI Security Lead | Contractual obligation in the requirements annex | Per the annex's maximum notification time |
| Executive and Board | Sev-1 within 1 hour to the CEO and the affected officers; the Audit Committee within 24 hours; **a 24-hour escalation call** for matters meeting the escalation criteria; quarterly reporting for all severities | Defined in GOV-PRO-006 | Per severity |

**Two communication failures recorded this period**, because the control's weaknesses matter more than its
design: a provider notified the entity of an event **later than its annex required** (recorded in the
supplier record, raised with the provider, and its assessment cadence increased to quarterly), and a customer
report arrived through a general support channel and took **4 hours** to be recognized as a security event —
which is why the support workflow now carries a mandatory security-flag question and Customer Operations has
a 1-hour raise obligation.

## 4. Review by those responsible for the security program

**Detected security events are reviewed by the individuals responsible for the management of the security
program.** Review is a defined obligation with a named reviewer per event class, and the review is recorded
so that "reviewed" is verifiable rather than assumed.

| Review | Reviewer | Cadence | Recorded output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual high-severity event | On-call engineer at triage; **AI Security Lead within the acknowledgement window** | Per event | Severity declaration with reasoning; IC assignment |
| **Every security event, including those closed at triage** | AI Security Lead | Weekly review of the full event queue | A sign-off that each closed-at-triage event was correctly closed — this exists because the events most likely to be mishandled are the ones nobody escalated |
| Break-glass uses | Compliance Manager and AI Security Lead | Reviewed within 1 business day; **all uses reviewed quarterly by the Audit Committee** | Justification recorded per use |
| Event trends, MTTD/MTTA/MTTR, the found-by-others proportion | AI Security Lead with the Compliance Manager | Monthly control review | Minuted conclusions and control changes |
| Events with confidentiality implications | AI Security Lead **and the data owner** | Per event | The §6 impact determination |
| Events with personal-data implications | **DPO, with the General Counsel** | Per event, within 1 hour of suspicion | The privacy impact determination and the notification decision (§7) |
| Events implicating a member of management | **Audit Committee, in camera without management present** | Per event | Committee minute; where warranted, external investigators appointed |
| Events implicating a provider | Third-Party Risk Manager with the AI Security Lead | Per event | Supplier record entry; corrective action; scope restriction where warranted |
| Quarterly security program review | AI Security Lead to the Audit Committee, and to the AI Governance Oversight Committee for model-related events | Quarterly | Reporting pack; Board action register entries |
| **Independent review of the security function's own handling** | Internal audit, reporting **without management present** | Annually | Findings into the deficiency register — the security function does not review itself (the compensating control for that segregation limitation) |

## 6. Analyzing security incidents and determining system impact

**The entity has procedures in place to analyze security incidents and determine system impact.** The
analysis is a required, structured phase with a defined output; an incident cannot proceed to closure with
the impact section unanswered, and **"unable to determine" is a permitted answer that escalates rather than
a blank that closes**.

**The impact determination template — every question is answered explicitly, including with "no" or "not
determinable":**

| Dimension | Questions answered | Sources of fact used |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope of systems** | Which systems, accounts, roles and network paths were reached? What could have been reached from there (lateral analysis), not merely what was? | Configuration inventory; identity graph; network path analysis; the IaC as the authoritative baseline |
| **Data** | Which data stores were accessible? Were any read, and by whom, and how is that known — from a log, or from an absence of a log? What classification? Whose tenant? | Access logs; storage access logs; query logs; the classification register |
| **Confidentiality** | §7 below — was customer confidential information used or disclosed? | Access logs; egress logs; DLP |
| **Personal data** | §8 below — was personal data involved, whose, which categories, and is a legal obligation triggered? | Data map; the personal-data inventory per store; DPO's assessment |
| **Integrity** | Was any data or configuration altered? **Was any assessment result altered or wrongly produced?** Can the result be re-derived and compared? | The absence of a human write path is itself part of the answer; result traceability to evidence and logic version; re-derivation |
| **Availability** | What was unavailable, for how long, against which commitment and tolerance? | Service metrics against the availability tolerance |
| **Root cause** | Why did it happen, and **why did the system permit it** — a human error is a symptom, not a root cause (the rule that caused one review to be returned as insufficient) | Timeline reconstruction; change records; the "five whys" required in the template |
| **Control analysis** | Which control failed, was absent, or was present but insufficient? Which control detected it — and if none did, why not? **Was the risk register wrong?** | The control register; the detection inventory; the risk register |
| **Attribution and intent** | Internal or external? Deliberate, negligent or accidental? Does the conduct process apply? Does law enforcement engagement apply? | Investigation evidence; kept separate from the technical timeline |
| **Provider involvement** | Was a provider or sub-processor implicated? What are its contractual notification and cooperation obligations, and were they met? | The requirements annex; the supplier record |
| **Evidence preservation** | Are logs, snapshots and images preserved before remediation destroys them, and for how long? | Preservation is a **step before eradication**, not after — with a legal-hold path via the General Counsel |
| **Blast-radius and recurrence** | Could the same cause exist elsewhere unfound? What sweep was run to check? | A mandatory "look for the same thing elsewhere" sweep, which is how the transport-security inconsistency across other buckets was found |

**Analysis capability and its limits.** Analysis draws on audit logging of the production account, storage
and query access logs, the managed SIEM's retained copy, endpoint telemetry, and the IaC baseline for
comparison. Two limitations are stated rather than left to be discovered: **the telemetry gap above meant
that for part of the period identity-resource change history was not captured by the configuration
recorder**, so an analysis covering that window would have had to rely on audit logs alone — this is exactly
why the gap was treated as a near miss with a mandatory review; and log retention on some stores was not
configured, which is why retention was set and log-configuration change alerting added. Where analysis
cannot reach a conclusion, the incident record says so, the DPO and General Counsel are told, and the
notification decision is taken on the **conservative assumption** rather than on the absence of proof.

**Every Sev-1, every Sev-2 with a control failure and every near miss ends in a post-incident review** with:
a factual timeline, the impact determination above, root cause, whether the risk register was wrong,
whether the control set needs to change, dated remediation with named owners, and closure verified by
someone other than the remediation owner. Reviews are blameless as to individuals and unsparing as to
systems — and the review of case D-2026-004 changed the system (local copies prevented) as well as handling
the individual.

## 7. Confidential information — assessing impact, identifying what was affected, and preventing recurrence

**Detected security events are evaluated to determine whether they could or did result in the unauthorized
disclosure or use of confidential information.** This evaluation is mandatory for every event above triage,
not only for events that look like data breaches, because the entity holds customers' own compliance
evidence — material whose disclosure would harm them commercially and which they gave the entity precisely
to have assessed.

**The confidentiality evaluation, performed by the AI Security Lead with the data owner:**

1. **What confidential information was within reach** of the systems, identities or paths involved — using
   the classification register and the per-store inventory, so the question is answered from a record rather
   than from memory.
2. **Whether it was accessed** — determined from access, storage and query logs. If logging for the relevant
   path was absent or incomplete, that is recorded as such and the evaluation proceeds on the conservative
   assumption that access may have occurred.
3. **Whether it left the environment** — egress and DLP records.
4. **Whose information** — which customer tenants, since multi-tenancy means an isolation failure is
   simultaneously a disclosure to a competitor; tenant isolation is a Critical control with a dedicated
   quarterly test objective for this reason.
5. **Whether use, as distinct from disclosure, occurred** — including use for a purpose outside the
   customer's agreement, which is the failure mode of an internal event like D-2026-004.
6. **A recorded conclusion** — disclosed / not disclosed / not determinable — with the evidence relied on.

**Identifying the affected information when unauthorized use or disclosure has occurred.** The procedure
requires identification to the level of the specific records and customers, not a category: the specific
evidence documents, assessment results, or fields; the customer tenants; the date range; the recipients if
known; and the basis for the determination. This is what makes notification accurate, and the entity commits
to naming what is not known as well as what is.

**Actions taken to prevent recurrence and address control failures.** For each of the two events this period
with a confidentiality dimension:

| Event | Affected confidential information identified | Action to address the control failure and prevent recurrence |
|---|---|---|
| **Case D-2026-004** — sample data copied to a local directory in breach of policy | Identified precisely: the specific sample data set, **entity-owned sample data containing no customer confidential information and no personal data** — confirmed from the data-set record and the access log, not assumed | Data deleted and deletion verified; handled under the conduct process; **the control failure was that the copy was possible at all**, so local copies of production and sample data are now technically prevented rather than prohibited; the case is taught as an anonymized study; retention and classification training updated |
| **Researcher disclosure** — inconsistent transport-security enforcement created a path by which data in transit was insufficiently protected on some routes | **No confidential information disclosed** — determined from the access logs for the affected paths over the full exposure window; the conclusion and its evidentiary basis are recorded, along with the fact that the window predated some retention improvements | Configuration corrected; the standard revised and made policy-as-code; **the remaining inconsistent buckets are recorded as an open deficiency**; log-configuration change alerting added so the evidence needed for a future determination cannot silently disappear |

**Confidentiality commitments and their consequences.** Where confidential information is or may be
disclosed, the affected customer is notified within 24 hours of confirmation with what is known and what is
not, the entity's contractual confidentiality commitments determine the remedies, and the incident is
reported to the Audit Committee. The entity's obligations flow through to providers: every provider with
access to confidential information holds confidentiality commitments **consistent with the entity's own**,
and their compliance is assessed rather than assumed.

## 8. Personal information — impact, legal compliance, identification and recurrence prevention

**Detected security events are evaluated to determine whether they could or did result in the unauthorized
disclosure or use of personal information, and whether there has been a failure to comply with applicable
laws or regulations.** The DPO is engaged **immediately on suspicion and in parallel with the security
response**, never after it concludes, and acknowledges within 1 hour. The DPO — not the security function —
owns the personal-data determination, and reports to a Board committee rather than to management, so the
determination cannot be shaped by the incident's owner.

**The privacy evaluation, performed by the DPO with the General Counsel:**

| Step | What is determined | Why it is separate from the security evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Personal data in scope | Which personal data was within reach, from the data map and the per-store personal-data inventory | Security's scope question is about systems; this one is about people |
| 2. Categories and sensitivity | Identity, contact, employment, screening, or special-category data; and **personal data incidentally present inside customer-supplied evidence**, which is a category the entity does not control the content of | Sensitivity drives both the legal test and the harm assessment |
| 3. Data subjects affected | Which individuals, how many, in which jurisdictions, and their relationship to the entity (personnel, customer personnel, data subjects of a customer) | Determines which laws apply and who must be told |
| 4. Controller or processor role | Whether the entity is controller (its own personnel data) or processor (customer evidence) — which determines whether the entity notifies a regulator or **notifies the customer so the customer can** | This distinction is the most consequential in the whole procedure and is decided explicitly, in writing |
| 5. **Failure to comply with law or regulation** | Whether the event constitutes or reveals a breach of an applicable obligation: security-of-processing requirements, breach-notification duties, transfer restrictions, retention limits, purpose limitation, or a contractual DPA obligation. Assessed against the **obligation inventory** maintained per jurisdiction across five geographies and two legal entities | A security event can be contained and still leave a legal non-compliance that must be recorded and, where required, notified |
| 6. Risk to individuals | Likelihood and severity of harm — the test that governs whether notification is required, distinct from harm to the entity | Prevents the entity's interest from driving the notification decision |
| 7. Notification determination | Regulator within **72 hours of awareness** where the threshold is met, with the clock started at discovery and **escalation at 50% elapsed**; individuals without undue delay where the threshold is met; the customer per its DPA where the entity is processor; **the decision is recorded with reasons whether or not notification is made** | A decision not to notify is a decision that must be defensible later |
| 8. Sub-processor dimension | Whether a sub-processor was involved, and whether its notification obligation was met | **R-006** — a sub-processor breach is a recognized register risk with its own runbook |

**Identifying the affected personal information.** Identification is to the record and individual level:
which individuals, which categories and fields, the date range, whether the data was encrypted and whether
the keys were also exposed, and where the information came from — including the case where personal data
sits inside a customer's evidence document. Where the entity cannot determine which individuals were
affected, that inability is itself recorded and the notification decision is taken on the conservative
basis; the entity does not treat an incomplete log as a negative finding.

**Instances and actions this period.**

| Case | Evaluation outcome | Legal-compliance assessment | Actions to prevent recurrence and address the control failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One suspected privacy incident**, reported by a colleague rather than by the person involved | Reported immediately on suspicion; **DPO acknowledged within the 1-hour commitment**; investigated | Assessed against the obligation inventory: **no personal-data breach and no notification threshold met** — determination recorded with its reasons and the evidence relied on | The control that worked was a colleague reporting on suspicion, which is reinforced in the quarterly briefing; the investigation confirmed the reporting route and the DPO response time in live conditions |
| Device loss (phone in a taxi) | Personal data possibility assessed by the DPO in parallel with the security response | **No personal data present**; encrypted and wiped 12 minutes after report; no obligation triggered | Reporting route simplified to a single phone number after the review found the email route added minutes |
| Case D-2026-004 (sample data) | Assessed for personal-data content | **Entity sample data; no personal data and no customer confidential information** — confirmed from the data-set record | Local copies technically prevented; retention and handling training updated; taught as an anonymized case study |
| **Retention conformance findings** — personal data held past its retention period | Not a disclosure event, but assessed by the DPO as a **compliance failure against the retention obligation** (**R-045**) | Recorded as a non-compliance with the retention requirement; assessed for notification and found not to meet the threshold; the assessment is recorded | Data deleted and the deletion evidenced **including at sub-processors**; the enforcement tightened so it does not recur; the quarterly retention conformance check retained as a detective control; upload-time and chat-time personal-data detection prompts kept in place |
| **Telemetry gap** (recorder scope and aggregator) assessed for privacy consequence | No personal-data disclosure; but the DPO recorded that the gap **would have impaired the entity's ability to meet a 72-hour notification obligation** had an event occurred in that window | Recorded as a compliance-capability deficiency, reported to the Audit Committee | Recording scope corrected; log retention set; detection inventory reconciliation added monthly — remediation driven by the privacy consequence, not only the security one |

**No statutory breach notification was required in the period.** That conclusion is the product of a
recorded assessment in each case, not the absence of one — and the two cases where the entity's *capability*
to notify was found wanting (the exercise's 31-hour decision and the telemetry gap) were remediated as
seriously as an actual breach would have been.

**Scope:** All legal, statutory, regulatory and contractual requirements relevant to **information

**Relationship to AIMS-REG-017.** AIMS-REG-017 *AI Reporting Obligations Register* is the register of
obligations to **report or share information** about the AI system, and remains authoritative for those.
This register is broader: it identifies every information-security requirement imposed on the organization
from outside, of which reporting duties are one domain. Where a requirement appears in both, AIMS-REG-017
holds the operative deadline and this register holds the control consequence; the semi-annual review
reconciles the two so a requirement cannot be current in one and stale in the other.

## 1. Purpose and standing rules

An external requirement that has been identified but not translated into a control is not satisfied, and
one that has been satisfied by accident is not evidenced. This register exists so that every external
requirement bearing on information security is written down once, owned by a named person, mapped to the
control that meets it, and re-verified on a cadence — and so that a change in the law becomes a change in
the control rather than a surprise at audit.

**Standing rules:**

- **Identified is not the same as met.** Every entry carries both the requirement and the control or
  document that satisfies it. An entry with no control reference is a **gap**, is recorded as such, and
  is treated as an open risk rather than as a completed identification.
- **The most demanding requirement governs.** Where several jurisdictions or contracts impose comparable
  duties at different levels, the organization implements the strictest and records that it has done so,
  rather than maintaining per-jurisdiction variants that diverge.
- **Silence is not permission.** Where the position is unclear, the General Counsel determines it in
  writing and the determination is recorded with its date and reasoning, so a later reader knows a
  decision was taken rather than a question overlooked.
- **A contractual duty is a real duty.** A commitment made to a customer is enforced with the same rigour
  as a statutory one; the register does not rank them.
- **Nothing is researched for the first time during an incident.** Every entry names its owner and, where
  a duty is time-bound, its clock.

**Completeness test applied at each review.** For each of the seven requirement **domains** in §3, the

which is the specific failure this test exists to catch.

## 4. How external requirements reach the controls

Identification is the input to the ISMS, not a parallel activity beside it. Each requirement is wired into
the specific ISMS process that has to change when the requirement changes, with a named owner and a
timeframe — so that a legal change produces a control change rather than a note.

| ISMS process | How the register feeds it | Owner | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Security policy development** | Every policy and standard carries, in its scope or governance section, the external requirements it exists to meet. The semi-annual review reconciles in **both directions**: every register entry names at least one document that satisfies it, and every policy's stated legal basis resolves to a current register entry. A new or changed requirement is assigned to a named document owner with a due date | General Counsel with CISO | Semi-annually and on change |
| **Control design and selection** | A new requirement is assessed for whether existing controls satisfy it, need extension, or need a new control. The determination is recorded per requirement — including a recorded determination that existing controls suffice, with the reasoning, so "already covered" is a decision and not an assumption | CISO | Within 30 days of a change taking effect |
| **Information classification and handling** | Legal duties attaching to a data category set its classification floor and its handling rules: personal data in evidence content is Restricted by legal duty, not by preference; a statutory retention period overrides a shorter lifecycle; a transfer restriction becomes a storage-location rule | DPO with CISO | On change; verified at each classification review |
| **Risk assessment and treatment** | Each register entry is a risk source with its own consequence of non-compliance (regulatory, contractual, or evidential). Legal and contractual consequence is an explicit input to impact scoring, and a compliance gap is entered on the risk register as a treatment item with an owner and a date — never left as an observation | CISO with General Counsel | At each risk review; immediately for a new gap |
| **Supplier requirements** | A duty we owe that depends on a supplier is flowed down as a contractual requirement before the supplier is engaged, and re-flowed at renewal (§6.1). A supplier who cannot accept a required term is not engaged for that scope, and the decision is recorded | Procurement with CISO | Pre-engagement and at renewal |
| **Contract terms we offer customers** | Commitments in customer contracts are made only where a control exists to meet them; the CISO reviews any security commitment before it is offered, so the organization does not contract to something it does not do | CISO with Legal | Per contract |
| **Change management** | A change that would breach a register entry is blocked at design review; the change record carries a compliance-impact field for changes touching data handling, retention, cryptography or transfer | Head of Engineering | Per change |
| **Internal audit programme** | Register entries with a legal or contractual consequence are in the audit universe, and the annual plan covers the entries whose failure would be most consequential | Internal Audit | Annually |
| **Awareness and training** | Duties that depend on individual behaviour become training content and contractual terms (HR-STD-046 §3.2), because a requirement met only by a control that a person can bypass is not met | CISO with Head of People | Annually and on change |
| **Management review** | Compliance status, open gaps, and forthcoming requirements are standing inputs | Chief AI Officer | At each review |

## 5. Cryptography: legal and regulatory compliance

Cryptography is regulated differently from other technology — its export, its use, and the ability of an
authority to compel access are each separately constrained, and the constraints differ by jurisdiction.
The organization's position is recorded here rather than assumed, and the General Counsel re-verifies it
semi-annually and before any change of deployment geography.

### 5.1 Import, export and transfer restrictions

| Aspect | Position |
|---|---|
| **What cryptography we use** | Platform-provided cryptography only: AWS KMS for key management, TLS 1.2+ for transit, AES-256 at rest, and standard signature and hashing algorithms. **No cryptography is developed in-house**, and no non-standard or proprietary algorithm is used — which materially narrows the export analysis and is a deliberate reason for the choice |
| **Export classification** | The service is not distributed as software to customers; it is operated as a hosted service, so no cryptographic product is exported by us. The cryptographic functionality reaching a customer is the platform provider's, under their classification. This determination is recorded by the General Counsel and re-verified semi-annually |
| **If distribution ever occurs** | Any change to a distributed-software model requires an export-classification assessment **before** release, by the General Counsel with external advice, covering classification, licence or exception availability, and reporting duties. This is a gate on the release, not a follow-up |
| **Import restrictions** | Jurisdictions that restrict the import or domestic use of strong cryptography are enumerated in the jurisdiction table; none of the current geographies imposes a restriction incompatible with the platform's defaults. A new geography is assessed for import restriction before the first customer there is onboarded |
| **Technology transfer to persons** | Access by personnel or suppliers located in a restricted jurisdiction is treated as a potential transfer; access is granted by location as well as by role, and the location is a recorded attribute of every Tier 3+ engagement |
| **Sanctions** | Customer and supplier onboarding includes a sanctions check; the account and region topology means no processing occurs in a sanctioned jurisdiction. Verified at onboarding and annually |
| **Deployment geography** | Primary region us-west-2; a new region is assessed for cryptographic, data-localisation and lawful-access implications before deployment, and the assessment is recorded |

### 5.2 Restrictions on use

| Aspect | Position |
|---|---|
| **Mandatory use** | Where law or contract requires encryption (GDPR Art 32 as an appropriate measure, customer contractual terms, PCI-adjacent handling), encryption is mandatory and its absence is a compliance gap, not a design choice. All customer evidence content is encrypted at rest with a customer-scoped KMS key and in transit |
| **Permitted strength** | The organization uses the strongest platform-supported standard configuration everywhere; no jurisdiction of operation currently caps permitted strength. A cap in a new jurisdiction is assessed before entry, and the outcome is either compliance or non-entry — never quiet non-compliance |
| **Key management duties** | Keys are managed in KMS with policies that separate duties: the roles that hold data cannot administer its key, and the masking and evidence keys deny every production role. Key rotation, retention and destruction follow the retention schedule, and a key whose data is under a statutory retention period or a legal hold **cannot be scheduled for deletion** — deletion of a key destroys the data as effectively as deleting the data, so it is governed by the same rule |
| **Prohibited use** | Personnel may not use unapproved cryptographic tooling on organizational information, and may not encrypt organizational data with a key the organization does not hold — because that converts a retention duty into an impossibility and is treated as a serious breach |
| **Cryptography as an obstruction risk** | Full-disk encryption on endpoints is mandatory, **and** key escrow is mandatory with it, so that an evidential acquisition remains possible (SEC-FOR-045 §5.1). Escrow availability is verified before any endpoint is powered off in an investigation |

### 5.3 Lawful access by authorities

| Aspect | Position |
|---|---|
| **Standing position** | The organization does not build or maintain any capability to defeat its own cryptography, and does not hold a mechanism to decrypt customer content outside the operational path. Where an authority compels production, the organization produces what it lawfully must, in the form in which it holds it |
| **Handling a request** | Every request is routed to the General Counsel; no engineer responds to a request directly. The request is assessed for validity, jurisdiction and scope; over-broad requests are narrowed or challenged; production is minimised to what is compelled; the request and response are recorded and retained. AIMS-REG-017 O-8 holds the operative process |
| **Notice to the affected party** | Where lawful, the affected customer or data subject is notified before production; where a non-disclosure requirement applies, the organization records that it applied and reviews it for expiry, so a gag is not treated as permanent by default |
| **Conflicts of law** | Where a lawful-access demand in one jurisdiction would breach a duty in another (a blocking statute, or a transfer restriction), the General Counsel obtains local advice and the conflict is escalated to the CEO. The conflict is recorded rather than resolved silently in favour of whichever authority asked most recently |
| **Transparency** | The number and type of requests received is reported to the AI Governance Board quarterly and, in summary form, in the annual transparency reporting under AIMS-REG-017 O-23. Current period: **0 lawful-access requests received** |

### 5.4 Validity of digital signatures, seals and certificates

| Aspect | Position |
|---|---|
| **Legal effect of electronic signatures** | Contracts and instruments — including the employment terms and contractor annexes in HR-STD-046 — are executed electronically. The General Counsel has determined the form of electronic signature required for each instrument class in each jurisdiction of use, and the platform in use meets it. Instruments requiring a qualified or witnessed form are identified and are **not** executed by the standard path |
| **Evidential weight of our own signatures** | Signed commits, artifact signatures and log-file validation digests are relied on as evidence of integrity and authorship. Their evidential value depends on key custody, so the signing keys' custody and access are recorded and the verification method is documented (SEC-FOR-045 §4.4) |
| **Certificate validity and lifecycle** | TLS certificates are issued through the managed platform authority with automated renewal; the certificate inventory is monitored for expiry with alarming ahead of it, because an expired certificate is both an outage and a prompt for users to accept an insecure connection. **0 unplanned certificate expiries** in the period |
| **Trust anchors** | The trusted-CA set on managed endpoints and in the runtime is managed, not ad hoc; a private CA's root is distributed by policy. Personnel may not add a trust anchor to a managed device |
| **Signature validity over time** | An electronic signature's verifiability outlasts the certificate that produced it only if the validation context is retained, so signed instruments are retained with their validation evidence — signature, certificate chain and timestamp — for the instrument's full retention period. A signature that cannot be validated in five years is not evidence in five years |
| **Time-stamping** | Records relied on evidentially carry a trusted timestamp from an NTP-synchronised source, with skew recorded (SEC-FOR-045 §4.3). Where a legal instrument requires a qualified timestamp, that requirement is identified per instrument class |
| **Long-term instruments** | Instruments with an indefinite duty (confidentiality, IP assignment) are additionally retained in a human-readable executed form, so their enforceability does not depend on a cryptographic verification path remaining available decades later |

## 6. Contractual requirements

Contractual duties are identified and managed in both directions: what we owe, and what we require of
others. Both are register entries, because a duty we owe that depends on a supplier is only as strong as
the term we imposed on them.

### 6.1 Requirements we impose on suppliers

Every supplier with access to organizational or customer information, or on whom a service dependency
rests, is engaged on terms that include the following. A term that cannot be obtained is a recorded
exception with a compensating control and named approval — not an omission.

| Required term | Why it is required | Verified |
|---|---|---|

| **Flow-down** to their personnel and their sub-processors, with named-individual annexes where the access is to customer data | Our duty to customers does not stop at our perimeter | Quarterly (4/4 annexes current) |

| **Incident notification within 24 hours** of their becoming aware, with the information we need to meet our own clocks | Our 72-hour and 4-hour clocks cannot start after theirs has run | Tested at the annual tabletop |
| **Audit rights** — the right to audit or to receive an independent assurance report, plus a right of on-site or remote inspection for suppliers holding customer data, plus the right to audit on cause following an incident | Assurance we cannot verify is assertion.

## 1. Documented results — summary of the assessment

The results of every AI system impact assessment are **documented** in a record of this form and
retained per §7. This record documents the current assessment in full: intended use and foreseeable
misuse (§2), positive and negative impacts (§3, §5), predictable failures and their mitigations (§4),
relevant demographic and other groups (§5.2), system complexity (§6.1), and the role of humans in
relation to the system (§6.2).

**Overall conclusion.** With the treatments in §4 and §8 in place, residual impact is **acceptable**.
Two residual impacts remain accepted-with-monitoring rather than eliminated: the possibility of a
plausible-but-wrong verdict (mitigated, not removed, by human oversight), and dependence on a supplier
model whose behaviour can change. Both are registered in the AI Risk Register (R-03, R-08) and
monitored in operation. No unacceptable impact was identified. The AIGB approved release on this basis.

## 2. Intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse

**Intended use.** Automated, evidence-cited assessment of an organization's compliance with a supported
managed control framework, from evidence that organization supplies and from authorized read-only
observation of its own cloud environment, to support — never replace — qualified human compliance
judgement.

**Reasonably foreseeable misuse** (assessed, with treatment; operational controls in AIMS-PRO-007 §7.1):

| Foreseeable misuse | Who could do it | Consequence if unchecked | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presenting a score as a certification or attestation | Assessed organization, to its own customers or a regulator | False assurance propagates downstream; the audit ecosystem is degraded | Explicit "assessment aid, not certification" statement on every result and in customer documentation; human review point disclosed |
| Rubber-stamping: accepting verdicts without the required human review | Assessed organization under time pressure | Real gaps go unremediated; oversight becomes nominal | Oversight point is documented and its exercise is logged; override and review rates monitored; analyst competence required (AIMS-REG-004) |
| Uploading a document that overstates compliance, to obtain a passing verdict | Assessed organization | Compliance theatre; assurance value destroyed | Where the scan directly reads the setting, observed technical state governs and a document cannot overwrite it; compensating controls cap at partial; evasion cases in the adversarial suite |
| Prompt injection through evidence content | Assessed organization, or an attacker who has compromised it | Verdicts manipulated at scale | Customer content is structurally data, never instructions; injection suite must be 100% blocked before release |
| Repurposing verdicts to evaluate individual employees | Assessed organization's management | Employment consequences from a system never validated for that use; human-rights concern | Prohibited in the intended-use statement; the system produces no individual-level determination; would require a new impact assessment |
| Running assessments to "shop" for a favourable result | Assessed organization | Selective disclosure to auditors | All assessments retained as an audit record; repeat runs visible; scan evidence always fresh |
| Scanning an environment without authority | Anyone able to configure an environment | Unauthorized access to a third party's account | Read-only credentials vended only where the customer established the trust relationship; every read attributable in CloudTrail |
| Using the system outside its characterized domain (unsupported framework or language) | Any user | Unquantified accuracy presented as quantified | Unsupported frameworks not offered; out-of-domain evidence routed to human review; limitation published |

## 3. Positive impacts

Assessed deliberately, because an impact assessment that records only harms cannot support a
proportionate decision.

| Positive impact | Beneficiary | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Compliance assessment becomes affordable and continuous rather than annual and sampled | Assessed organizations, especially smaller ones without a compliance function | Turnaround falls from weeks to minutes at a fraction of manual cost |
| Real AI-governance practice is raised, not just documented | Society, users of AI systems generally | Concrete, per-requirement findings are harder to satisfy with paperwork than a self-attestation is |
| Findings are evidence-cited and reproducible, so they are contestable | Assessed organizations; auditors | Every verdict cites the document excerpt or raw API record behind it |
| Consistency between assessors improves | Assessed organizations; the audit ecosystem | The same rubric is applied to every organization, removing assessor-to-assessor variance |
| Skilled human effort moves from evidence-gathering to judgement | Compliance analysts and auditors | Automation targets collection and collation, not the judgement step |
| Gaps are found that manual sampling misses | End users of assessed organizations | Full-population scanning rather than sampling |

## 6. System characteristics considered

### 6.1 System complexity

A five-node graph with tool use, autonomous read-only scanning of a live cloud account, two parallel
evidence tracks, a model-authored judgement step on an Opus-class model, and deterministic scoring in
code. Complexity is **material to impact** in three ways: (a) failure can arise in orchestration rather
than in the model, so testing covers the whole path end-to-end, not the model alone; (b) more
autonomous steps mean more places for injected content to act, so the trust boundary is structural;
(c) explanation must cover the chain, not one inference, which is why the assessment journal exposes
every phase and tool call. Complexity is deliberately bounded: anything that must be reproducible —
routing, scoring, coverage validation — is implemented in code, not left to a model.

### 6.2 Role of humans in relation to the system

| Role | Human involvement |
|---|---|
| Before assessment | Humans define scope, register environments, supply and link evidence, and authorize read access |
| During assessment | No human in the loop — the run is autonomous and machine-invoked; a human may halt it |
| **Before consequence** | **A qualified compliance analyst reviews verdicts and may override any of them. This is the decisive oversight point: the AI system never holds final authority over a compliance outcome** |
| After publication | Humans annotate, contest, re-run with new evidence, and act on remediation tasks |
| Over the system | Operators may suspend the system; the AIGB may refuse or withdraw it |
| Accountability | Named accountable roles per AIMS-POL-003; the system is never the accountable party |

**Known dependency:** the mitigation for the most serious residual impact (a plausible-but-wrong
verdict) is human review — so oversight quality is itself monitored, because a mitigation that decays
in practice is not a mitigation.

## 7. Societal impacts

The organization **assesses and documents the potential societal impacts of its AI systems throughout
the life cycle**, considering both **beneficial and detrimental** effects.

### 7.1 Impact areas

| Societal impact area | Assessment | Direction | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Environmental sustainability** | Inference is computationally significant. Estimated at the order of tens of thousands of tokens per control assessment; an Opus-class model is used for the judgement node only, precisely to contain this. Set against the organization's environmental sustainability goals: this is a cloud-only workload in a region with a published carbon profile, and it **displaces** travel and manual audit effort, which is a partial offset | Both | Inference cost and estimated emissions per assessment tracked release over release (RD-8, IC-12); a regression is treated as a defect; smaller models used wherever they meet the accuracy bar; assessments are not re-run without cause; results cached rather than recomputed. Reported to the AIGB quarterly against the organization's sustainability strategy |
| **Economic impacts** | Beneficial: lowers the cost of assurance, which disproportionately helps organizations that cannot afford a compliance function; shifts skilled labour from collation to judgement. Detrimental: reduces demand for junior audit and evidence-gathering work, and could concentrate assurance capability in whoever owns the tooling | Both | Automation scoped to collation, not judgement; findings are contestable and portable so customers are not locked in; task displacement tracked as a worker impact (§5.2) |
| **Government and political impacts** | The system produces material that regulators and certification bodies may rely on. Beneficial: better-evidenced compliance conversations. Detrimental: if a score were mistaken for an attestation, it could distort regulatory judgement or be used to lobby for weaker supervision; a systematic bias in the rubric could shift practice across a whole sector | Both | Explicit non-certification disclosure; rationale always published with the verdict so a regulator can check the reasoning; rubric changes are Material changes with communication; no political or policy-advocacy use of aggregate customer data |
| **Health and safety impacts** | No direct effect — the system controls no physical process and makes no clinical decision. **Indirect** effect considered: if a safety-relevant AI governance gap were missed in an organization operating a safety-critical AI system, harm could propagate downstream | Detrimental (indirect) | Gap recall held to the stricter threshold precisely because a missed gap propagates; safety-relevant frameworks flagged for mandatory human review |
| **Impacts on norms, traditions, culture and values** | Two real effects. (a) **Normalizing automated judgement of governance** — shifting the professional norm from human assessor judgement toward machine-first assessment, and shifting what "compliance" means toward what a machine can read. (b) **Cultural and linguistic narrowness** — an English-language, Western-framework-trained rubric could impose one documentation culture as the standard of adequacy, disadvantaging organizations whose legitimate practice is expressed differently (oral process knowledge, differently structured governance, other languages) | Detrimental | Human judgement retained as the final authority by design, so the professional norm is augmented rather than replaced; rubrics score substance, not documentation style; the English-only limitation is published; non-English evidence routed to human review; corpus rebalanced annually; requirement interpretation reviewed with practising analysts each quarter |
| **Effects on the assurance ecosystem** | Beneficial if it raises the floor on real governance practice; detrimental if it enables **compliance theatre** at scale — the most consequential societal risk identified | Both | IC-10: a document alone cannot assert away an observed misconfiguration; observed technical state governs where the scan reads it; compensating controls capped below full compliance; evasion cases in the adversarial suite; assessments retained as an audit record |
| **Concentration of information** | Aggregate visibility into many organizations' governance postures is itself sensitive | Detrimental | Tenant isolation; no aggregate customer data used for any purpose beyond the customer's own assessment without explicit contractual permission |

### 7.2 Misuse to create societal harm, and reinforcement of historical biases

The organization **analyses how actors could misuse the system to create societal harm and how it could
reinforce unwanted historical social biases.**

| Actor | Misuse pathway | Societal harm | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assessed organization | Publishing a score as an attestation to customers, insurers or a regulator | Systemic false assurance; erosion of trust in AI governance claims generally | Non-certification disclosure; rationale published; audit record retained |
| Assessed organization | Crafting documentation to pass rather than to govern | Compliance theatre at scale; the standard becomes a paperwork exercise | Observed technical state governs where read; compensating controls capped; evasion suite |
| Attacker who has compromised a customer | Injecting content to manufacture compliant verdicts | Undetected non-compliance in critical infrastructure | Structural trust boundary; injection suite as a release gate |
| Management within an assessed organization | Repurposing verdicts to assess individual employees | Unjustified employment consequences from an unvalidated use | Prohibited use; no individual-level output; new impact assessment required |
| A vendor of this class of tool (including us) | Marketing automated assessment as sufficient, discouraging human expertise | De-skilling of the assurance profession; oversight becomes nominal | Human authority retained by design; oversight health monitored; honest limitation statements in customer-facing documentation |
| Any actor | Using aggregate posture data to target or pressure organizations | Confidentiality harm; chilling effect on honest self-assessment | Tenant isolation; no secondary use of customer data |

**Reinforcement of unwanted historical social biases — analysed:**

| Historical bias that could be reinforced | Mechanism in this system | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Privileging formal, Western, English-language documentation practice** as the marker of competence | Corpus and rubrics are English and drawn from Western-origin frameworks; polished documentation may read as more compliant | Rubrics score required elements, not fluency or style; terse-compliant fairness stratum; English-only limitation published; human review for other languages; annual corpus rebalancing |
| **Advantaging large, well-resourced organizations** over small ones, replaying the historical pattern that assurance is a privilege of scale | A dedicated compliance function produces evidence that scores well; a small team's real controls may be less legible | Substance-over-polish rubric; low cost per assessment is itself a levelling effect; disparity across maturity strata investigated before release |
| **Inherited bias in the underlying foundation models** — including associations attached to names, regions or languages appearing in evidence | Model judgement is the mechanism; we do not control the weights | Fairness-strata testing on our actual task; no demographic attributes held or inferred; anomalous disparity investigated as a defect; supplier obligations recorded (AIMS-PRO-010) |
| **Devaluing non-documentary forms of knowledge** (oral process, tacit practice) | Only written and API-observable evidence can be read | Published as a limitation; human review can accept evidence the system cannot read; the system never asserts non-compliance from absence of a readable source (RD-2) |

**Addressing historical harms.** The system is also assessed for how it can be used to *reduce* an
existing inequity: the historical concentration of assurance capability in organizations that could
afford professional auditors. Lowering the cost of credible, evidence-cited assessment by an order of
magnitude extends real governance capability to organizations previously priced out — recorded as an
intended beneficial societal effect, and one reason the AIGB approved the system.

## 9. Communication of results to users and interested parties

The documented results **support communication** and are used for it. Communicated externally, in
customer-facing documentation and release notes: intended purpose; the non-certification statement; the
technical limitations in AIMS-DOC-012 §2; the human oversight requirement; the domain of use and its
English-language limitation; and verdict-affecting changes ahead of their effective date. Communicated
internally: this record in full, to the AIGB and the accountable roles. Communicated on request, under
NDA: the full record to external auditors and certification bodies. Not communicated: prompts and
rubrics, and any tenant-identifying content.

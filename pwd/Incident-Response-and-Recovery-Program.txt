# Incident Response and Recovery Program

**Document ID:** SEC-IRP-027
**Version:** 5.1 — **effective 2026-03-23**, superseding v4.6 of 2025-10-13
**Owner:** CISO (program owner); Head of Security Engineering (operational execution)
**Approved by:** CISO 2026-03-18; reviewed by the General Counsel 2026-03-17 for the breach-notification
provisions
**Next scheduled review:** Annually (next by 2026-12-31), after every Severity 1 or 2 incident, and after every
recovery test

**Scope.** Understanding, containing, mitigating, remediating, recovering from, communicating about, and learning
from security, availability, processing-integrity, confidentiality, and privacy incidents. Applies to the
production service, the supporting infrastructure, the build and supply chain, endpoints, and incidents
originating at a third party that affect the entity's commitments. The reference implementation is AWS account
**471885797689**, region **us-west-2**.

Companion documents: the security monitoring procedure (how an incident is detected and declared); the
configuration standards and vulnerability management procedure (remediation deadlines); the business continuity
and recovery testing plan (availability disruption scenarios); the data-subject rights and third-party
disclosure procedures (privacy obligations); the change management policy (the route by which a remediation
reaches production).

## 1. Roles and responsibilities (CC7.4-R1)

### 1.1 Internal roles

| Role | Assigned to | Responsibility in an incident |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive sponsor** | Chief Executive | Accountable for the entity's response; authorises external communication and any decision with material business consequence |
| **Program owner** | CISO | Owns the design, maintenance, and effectiveness of this program; declares Severity 1; decides on external forensic engagement; owns notification decisions jointly with the General Counsel |

| **Legal counsel** | General Counsel | Regulatory notification obligations, privilege, preservation duties, contractual notice, law-enforcement liaison |
| **Communications lead** | Head of Marketing & Communications | Customer, public, and internal messaging, consistent with §6 |
| **Customer liaison** | Head of Customer Success | Named-customer notification and the ongoing status relationship |
| **Human Resources** | Head of People | Where an employee or contractor is implicated; owns the conduct process and sanctions under §8.3 |

Every role has a **named primary and a named alternate**, both recorded in the on-call system with out-of-hours
contact paths validated monthly. No role depends on one individual — the recovery tests in §7 include a scenario
in which the primary is unavailable.

### 1.2 External resources, engaged when internal capability is exceeded

| Resource | Purpose | Engagement route |
|---|---|---|
| Retained digital forensics and incident response firm | Deep forensic analysis, adversary attribution, surge capacity | Master services agreement with a **pre-agreed response-time commitment**; engaged by the CISO without needing a new procurement cycle |
| External breach counsel | Regulatory notification analysis across jurisdictions; privilege | Retainer; engaged by the General Counsel |
| Cloud provider security and support | Provider-side investigation, log retrieval beyond entity visibility, account-level containment | Support relationship; escalation path recorded in the runbook |

| Law enforcement | Where criminal activity is indicated | Via the General Counsel only |
| Customer and vendor security contacts | Coordinated response where an incident crosses an organisational boundary | Contact details maintained in the customer and supplier registers |

The retainers exist **before** they are needed, because negotiating a forensic engagement during an active
incident is itself a failure mode. Contact details are verified annually.

## 2. Understanding the incident: nature, severity, and scope (CC7.4-R7, CC7.4-R8)

### 2.1 Declaration and initial understanding

An incident is declared from a security event under the monitoring procedure's criteria. Within the initial
triage window the commander establishes, and the scribe records: what was observed and by which detection; the
**method of occurrence** as far as it is then known; which system resources, data stores, identities, and
credentials are affected or in reach; whether the activity is ongoing; whether personal, confidential, or
customer data is engaged; and which entity objectives and commitments are threatened.

The audit trail is the primary instrument for this. Because the trail records identity, source, parameters, and
outcome for every control-plane call, and is protected by log file validation and a customer-managed key with a
policy that excludes operational identities from deletion, the reconstruction of "what did this principal
actually do" is evidentiary rather than inferential. Data-plane access logs on all 13 buckets and flow logs on the
in-scope VPC extend the same reconstruction to data access and network activity.

**Understanding is provisional and revised.** The initial scope is explicitly recorded as provisional; scope is
re-assessed at every status checkpoint, and a scope expansion re-triggers the severity assessment. An incident is
never closed on the initial scope alone.

### 2.2 Severity and response time frames

| Severity | Definition | Response time frame |
|---|---|---|
| **Sev 1** | Confirmed unauthorized access to customer or personal data; confirmed malware or unauthorized code in production; loss of audit integrity; complete service unavailability; ransomware; compromise of the break-glass or root credential | Commander assigned and response underway **within 30 minutes**, 24×7; executive sponsor and General Counsel notified within 1 hour; status checkpoints hourly |
| **Sev 2** | Credible indicator of compromise without confirmed data access; compromise of a single non-privileged credential; partial or degraded service; unauthorized component in production; confirmed control bypass | Underway **within 2 hours**, 24×7; checkpoints every 4 hours |
| **Sev 3** | Contained security weakness actively being exploited at low impact; isolated endpoint malware detection successfully quarantined; single-tenant processing error | Underway **within 1 working day**; daily checkpoints |
| **Sev 4** | Policy violation without exploitation; unexplained but bounded anomaly requiring investigation | Underway **within 3 working days**; reviewed weekly |

Severity is assigned from **impact and reach, not from the volume of alerts**, and is re-assessed at every
checkpoint — it may be raised or lowered, and every change is recorded with its reason. Malware affecting
production or customer data is Sev 1 by default. Where severity is genuinely ambiguous, the incident is run at
the **higher** severity until evidence supports lowering it.

## 3. Containment (CC7.4-R2, CC7.4-R9)

### 3.1 Determining the containment approach

Containment is a **decision with a stated trade-off**, not a reflex. For each incident the commander chooses
between immediate isolation and monitored containment, weighing the rate of ongoing harm, the evidentiary value
of continued observation, the availability impact of isolation, and the risk that the adversary detects the
response and escalates. The decision, its rationale, and its time are recorded. Default: **when customer or
personal data is in reach and actively accessible, isolate immediately** and accept the loss of observation.

### 3.2 The containment actions available

| Vector | Containment action | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Compromised credential or session | Deactivate the credential; invalidate sessions; attach a deny-all policy where deletion would destroy evidence | Revoke first, investigate second. The already-inactive retired build credential in the reference account carries exactly this deny-all pattern as a standing example |
| Compromised runtime component | Replace the task from a known-good artifact digest; revoke the component's role credentials | The runtime is immutable, so replacement is the normal operation, not an emergency measure |
| Compromised endpoint | Network-isolate via EDR; preserve, then re-image | Full-disk encryption limits confidentiality impact of physical loss |
| Network-borne activity | Tighten security group and network rules to default-deny; **VPC Block Public Access is already `block-bidirectional`**, which bounds the exposure before any incident begins | Pre-existing posture is the cheapest containment |
| Unauthorized configuration change | Revert by redeploying the infrastructure-as-code definition; identify the actor from the trail | Drift reconciliation confirms the revert held |
| Data exfiltration path | Remove the egress channel; apply a restrictive bucket or key policy; disable the credential that authorised the read | Object-store access logs establish what was read |
| Supply-chain compromise | Quarantine the artifact and the dependency version; block the version in the curated repository; redeploy the last known-good digest | Bill of materials identifies every affected release |
| Vendor-side compromise | Suspend the vendor's access from the entitlement record; search the trail for its use in the exposure window | Enumerate from records, not from memory |
| Audit-integrity concern | Preserve the archive; verify log file validation digests; restrict all mutation on the archive | No operational identity holds delete permission on the archive |
| Account-level compromise | Engage the provider's account-level containment; use break-glass only under the two-person rule, which itself raises `AIGov-RootAccountUsage` | The alarm firing during a sanctioned response is expected and is recorded in the timeline |

### 3.3 Evidence preservation during containment

Before remediation destroys state, and where feasible without prolonging harm: capture volatile memory and disk
images from affected endpoints, snapshot affected volumes, export the relevant trail and access-log windows to
preservation storage, and record hashes of everything captured with a chain of custody. Legal counsel issues a
**preservation notice** for Sev 1 and Sev 2 incidents, suspending routine deletion for the relevant data. In the
immutable runtime, the replaced task is captured before termination where the platform permits; where it cannot
be, that limitation is recorded in the timeline rather than left as an unexplained gap.

## 4. Mitigating ongoing effects and remediating (CC7.4-R3, CC7.4-R4, CC7.4-R10, CC7.4-R11, CC8.1-R11)

### 4.1 Mitigating while the incident is live

Containment stops the mechanism; mitigation limits the consequences that are already in motion. Concurrent with
containment: revoke every credential the affected component could have reached, not only the one known to be
used; rotate secrets and keys in the blast radius; assess and bound the exposure using the entitlement and asset
records; heighten monitoring on the affected identities, resources, and data paths for the duration and for a
defined watch period afterward; notify affected internal teams so that dependent decisions are not made on
compromised assumptions; and, where processing integrity is affected, **quarantine the affected outputs so that
downstream consumers do not act on them** while correction is in progress.

### 4.2 Remediating: closing the vulnerability and removing unauthorized access

Resolution requires all of the following to be evidenced before an incident may be closed:

1. **The vulnerability is closed** — the specific weakness that permitted the incident is fixed, not merely
   worked around. Where a permanent fix takes longer than the containment can safely hold, an interim
   compensating control is put in place with an owner and a dated plan, and the incident remains open.
2. **Unauthorized access is removed** — every credential, session, key, policy grant, trust relationship,
   network path, and persistence mechanism the adversary held or could have created is enumerated and removed.
   Enumeration is from records and the trail, not recollection.
3. **Configuration is returned to baseline** — the affected resources evaluate compliant against the continuous
   rule set (**368 rules**), allowing for the up-to-24-hour periodic evaluation latency, which is noted in the
   timeline rather than treated as an unresolved finding.
4. **Artifact integrity is confirmed** — the running digest matches the approved release.
5. **Detection coverage is confirmed or added** — if the incident was found by something other than a detection,
   or later than it should have been, a detection work item is raised before closure.

**All remediation reaches production through the change management process.** Identification of a needed change
during an incident **initiates the change process immediately**, using the emergency path where the urgency
requires: a second person's concurrence at the time, and full documentation within one working day. Emergency
changes compress the sequence but do not omit approval, testing where feasible, or the record. Incident-derived
changes are tagged as such so that the weekly change review can examine them as a set.

### 4.3 Documenting and communicating remediation

Every incident carries a record containing: the timeline with times and actors; the detections that fired and
when; severity assignments and every change to them; the method of occurrence; the scope of affected resources
and data; every containment, mitigation, and remediation action with its actor and time; the evidence preserved
and its custody; the notification determinations and every communication issued; the root cause; the corrective
actions with owners and due dates; and the closure approval. Sev 1 and Sev 2 records are approved by the CISO at
closure; Sev 1 records are reported to the Audit Committee and to the executive sponsor. Remediation activities
are communicated to affected internal teams at each checkpoint, to affected customers per §6, and to the Audit
Committee quarterly in aggregate.

## 5. Restoration and recovery (CC7.4-R5, CC7.5-R1)

### 5.1 Restoring to an interim operating state

The objective immediately after containment is a state in which entity objectives can be met, which may be
degraded relative to normal. Options, in order of preference: redeploy the last known-good artifact digest to
clean infrastructure; fail over to an unaffected component or region; restore data from a verified backup to the
last known-good point using point-in-time recovery; or operate a reduced-function mode with the affected capability
disabled. **Interim states are recorded as such, with an owner and a target date for full restoration**, so that
a temporary posture does not silently become permanent.

### 5.2 Full restoration

| Restoration activity | How it is performed |
|---|---|
| Rebuilding systems | From infrastructure-as-code and an approved artifact digest — never by cleaning a compromised instance in place. Endpoints are re-imaged, not repaired |
| Updating software and installing patches | Through the pipeline as a normal change; because the runtime is immutable, patching is a redeploy |
| Modifying access controls | Entitlements revised per the least-privilege review the incident triggered; changes reviewed by an owner other than the requester |
| Changing configurations | Baseline updated in infrastructure-as-code first, so the corrected configuration is the durable intent and not a console change that drift will later overwrite |
| Restoring data | From backups whose restorability is proven by the testing regime in the backup standard, to a validated point in time, with integrity verification before return to service |
| Rotating cryptographic material | Keys and secrets in the blast radius rotated; the customer-managed trail key's rotation schedule is unaffected but rotation can be forced |
| Re-validating before return to service | Configuration rules compliant; detections healthy and firing; artifact digest verified; functional and integrity checks passed; the specific vulnerability confirmed closed |

Return to service is a **decision by the incident commander with the service owner's concurrence**, recorded with
the evidence relied upon. A heightened monitoring watch period follows every Sev 1 and Sev 2 restoration, with its
duration set by the commander and a minimum of **14 days**.

## 6. Communication (CC7.4-R6, CC7.5-R2)

### 6.1 Communication matrix

| Audience | Trigger | Timing | Owner | Content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incident team | Declaration | Immediate | Commander | Full detail |
| Executive sponsor and CISO | Sev 1; Sev 2 on escalation | Within 1 hour of Sev 1 | Commander | Impact, containment status, decisions needed |
| General Counsel | Any incident with possible data, regulatory, or contractual implication | Within 1 hour of Sev 1 | Commander | Facts for the notification analysis |
| Affected customers | Confirmed or reasonably suspected impact to their data or service | Without undue delay, and within any contractual notice period — **72 hours** is the default outer bound absent a shorter contractual term | Customer liaison, content approved by Legal and Communications | What happened, what data or service was affected, what the entity is doing, what the customer should do, and how to get updates |
| All customers | Material service disruption | Status updates at each checkpoint | Communications lead | Status, scope, expected restoration |
| Data subjects | Confirmed unauthorized use or disclosure of personal information, where the risk threshold in §8.2 is met | Without undue delay, per the applicable statutory deadline | Privacy lead, approved by Legal | Nature of the breach, categories of data, likely consequences, mitigation measures, contact point, and steps the individual can take |
| Supervisory authorities and regulators | Per the jurisdictional analysis in §8.1 | Per the applicable statutory deadline — **72 hours from awareness** where that regime applies | General Counsel | As prescribed by the regime |
| Sub-processors and vendors | Where their environment or data is implicated | Promptly | Commander | Scope and required action |
| Cyber insurer | Per policy terms | Per policy terms | General Counsel | As prescribed |
| Law enforcement | Where criminal activity is indicated and disclosure is appropriate | At the General Counsel's direction | General Counsel | As appropriate |
| Internal workforce | Sev 1, and any incident with behavioural lessons | At closure, or earlier if action is required | Communications lead | Sanitised account and required actions |
| Audit Committee | Sev 1 individually; all incidents in aggregate | Sev 1 promptly; otherwise quarterly | CISO | Incidents, root causes, corrective actions, trends |

### 6.2 Communication principles

Communications state **what is known, what is not yet known, and when the next update will come**. Speculation is
excluded; a correction is issued promptly and prominently if a statement turns out to be wrong. A single approved
channel and a single spokesperson are used per audience to prevent divergent accounts. Pre-drafted templates exist
for each audience so that drafting is not on the critical path, and the templates are exercised in the tests in
§7. Communications about recovery actions and about the measures taken to prevent recurrence are issued at
closure, internally and — for incidents that were externally visible or notified — externally.

## 7. Root cause, learning, and testing (CC7.5-R3 to CC7.5-R10, CC7.4-R12, CC7.4-R13)

### 7.1 Root cause and corrective action

Every incident at Sev 3 and above gets a post-incident review within **10 working days** of closure, chaired by
someone who was **not the incident commander**. It is explicitly blameless as to individuals and explicitly
unforgiving as to systems. It determines:

- The **root cause**, pursued past the proximate trigger to the systemic reason — a missing control, a control
  that existed but did not operate, a process that permitted the state, or a design decision that made the failure
  possible. "Human error" is never accepted as a root cause; the question is why the system permitted the error
  to have that effect.
- **Which preventive control failed or was absent**, and what preventive or detective control is added or changed
  as a result (CC7.5-R4). This produces concrete architecture or control changes — additional detections, a
  tightened policy, a new automated gate, a segmentation change — each raised through change management.
- **Whether detection performed as designed** (CC7.2-R12): did it fire, at what stage of the attack chain, and how
  could it have fired earlier. An incident found by a human or a customer rather than a detection is itself a
  finding.
- **The timeline against the target response times** in §2.2, with an explanation for any target missed.
- **Corrective actions**, each with a single named owner and a due date, entered into the deficiency register and
  tracked to closure. Overdue corrective actions escalate to the CISO and are reported to the Audit Committee.

### 7.2 Periodic review of incident patterns and program design

| Review | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Incident pattern review across security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy incidents | **Quarterly** | Identify recurring root causes and the **system changes** they imply (CC7.4-R13). A cause recurring across incidents is a systemic finding regardless of the low severity of each instance |
| Program design effectiveness evaluation | **Annually**, and after every Sev 1 | Evaluate whether the design of the response activities — roles, severities, time frames, escalation, communication, retainers — is adequate for the current threat landscape and architecture (CC7.4-R12) |
| Corrective action closure review | Monthly | Confirm actions are closing, not merely being logged |
| Lessons-learned incorporation | After each test and each incident | Update this program, the runbooks, and the recovery procedures (CC7.5-R5) |

### 7.3 Recovery plan testing (CC7.5-R6 to CC7.5-R10)

Testing runs at least **semi-annually**, alternating tabletop exercises with technical simulations, plus a
recovery test after any material change to the architecture or the response team.

**Scenario selection is risk-driven (CC7.5-R7):** scenarios are chosen from the threat register by the product of
**likelihood and magnitude**, so the exercise set reflects what is actually likely to happen and what would hurt
most, and the selection rationale is recorded. The current standing set:

| Scenario | Threat basis | What it exercises |
|---|---|---|
| Credential compromise leading to data access | High likelihood; high magnitude | Revocation, trail-based scope reconstruction, breach determination, customer and data-subject notification |
| Ransomware or destructive action on stored data | Moderate likelihood; severe magnitude | Backup restoration to a validated point, integrity verification, interim operating state |
| Supply-chain compromise in a dependency | Moderate likelihood; high magnitude | Bill-of-materials exposure query, artifact quarantine, rollback to known-good digest |
| Insider misuse of a privileged entitlement | Lower likelihood; high magnitude | `AIGov-*` alarm triage, two-person break-glass, HR and conduct process, evidence handling |
| Regional or provider service loss | Lower likelihood; severe magnitude | Failover, degraded-mode operation, customer communication at scale |
| Processing-integrity failure producing incorrect assessment output | Moderate likelihood; moderate magnitude | Output quarantine, correction and re-issue, notification of affected consumers |
| Audit-log tampering or loss | Low likelihood; severe magnitude | Log file validation verification, preservation, provider escalation |

**Cross-entity component coverage (CC7.5-R8):** each test enumerates the components relevant to the availability
impairment being simulated and confirms each is exercised or explicitly scoped out with a reason — the runtime,
the record store, the object store, the key management dependency, the identity dependency, the audit and
monitoring estate, the build pipeline, the endpoint fleet, third-party dependencies, and the human processes
(on-call, communications, decision authority). A component never included in any test within a rolling 12 months
is a coverage gap raised as a finding.

**Key-personnel unavailability (CC7.5-R9):** at least one test per year is run with the primary incident
commander, the primary on-call engineer, and one executive role declared unavailable at the start, with no advance
warning to the alternates. The test's purpose is to prove the alternates can execute from the documented
runbooks — if the exercise only succeeds because someone's undocumented knowledge was reachable, that is the
finding.

**Revising the posture on results (CC7.5-R10):** every test produces a report with findings, and findings become
corrective actions with owners and due dates. Where results show that a recovery objective is not achievable as
designed, the **resilience posture and continuity plans are revised** — the objective is corrected or the
architecture is changed, and the choice is made explicitly by the CISO and the service owner rather than left as
an aspirational number. Revisions flow into the business continuity plan and, where they require engineering
work, into the roadmap with executive visibility.

## 8. Privacy breach provisions (CC7.4-R14, CC7.4-R15, CC7.4-R16)

### 8.1 Breach response procedure for a confirmed privacy incident

When an incident is confirmed to involve personal information, the privacy breach procedure runs **in addition to**
everything above, owned by the Privacy lead with the General Counsel:

1. **Confirm and classify** — establish which categories of personal information, which data subjects, which
   jurisdictions, and which volume, using the data inventory and classification from the asset inventory
   standard and the access evidence from the trail and data-plane logs.
2. **Determine controller/processor posture** — where the entity processes personal information on a customer's
   behalf, the entity is a processor and its primary duty is to notify that controller **without undue delay**,
   supplying the information the controller needs for its own regulatory notification. Where the entity is the
   controller, it notifies authorities and data subjects directly. This determination is made and recorded
   explicitly, because it dictates who notifies whom.
3. **Assess risk to individuals** — likelihood and severity of harm, considering sensitivity, identifiability,
   volume, whether the data was encrypted or otherwise unintelligible, and whether the recipient is known.
4. **Notification analysis per jurisdiction** — the General Counsel determines the applicable regimes, thresholds,
   deadlines, and content requirements. Where a **72-hour-from-awareness** obligation applies, the clock is
   recorded from the moment of awareness, and a notification is made on the information available rather than
   delayed for completeness, with a supplementary notification to follow.
5. **Notify** — per §6.1, with content per §8.2.
6. **Record** — a breach register entry is created for **every** confirmed privacy incident, including those
   assessed as not notifiable, with the reasoning for the non-notification decision. Regulators may ask to see
   the decisions that led to silence as readily as the ones that led to notice.
7. **Support affected individuals** — a contact point, and where warranted, monitoring or protective services.
8. **Remediate and learn** — as §4 and §7.

### 8.2 Communicating unauthorized use or disclosure of personal information (CC7.4-R15)

Notification to data subjects, authorities, and other required recipients is made when the assessment in §8.1.3
indicates a risk to individuals or when a regime requires notification irrespective of risk. Content includes: the
nature of the breach; the categories and approximate number of individuals and records; the likely consequences;
the measures taken or proposed, including mitigation; a contact point for further information; and the steps
individuals can take to protect themselves. Notification is direct where contact details exist and by public
communication where individual notice is disproportionate or impossible. Customers who are controllers are given
what they need to discharge their own obligations, and the entity does not notify a controller's data subjects
without that controller's coordination.

### 8.3 Evaluating conduct and applying sanctions (CC7.4-R16)

Where an unauthorized use or disclosure of personal information involves the conduct of an individual or an
organisation, the conduct is evaluated:

| Party | Evaluation | Consequence range |
|---|---|---|
| Employee or contractor | Investigation by Human Resources with Security Engineering, respecting due process and the individual's rights; the factual record comes from the audit trail and access logs, not from supposition | Coaching and retraining; formal warning; removal of entitlements; suspension; termination; referral to law enforcement where criminal conduct is indicated. Applied consistently with employment law and the disciplinary policy, which the workforce acknowledges at onboarding and annually |
| Vendor, sub-processor, or other organisation | Assessment against contractual security and privacy obligations; request for their incident report and corrective actions; re-assessment of their risk tier | Mandated remediation with deadlines; suspension of access or of processing; contractual remedies; escalation to termination and offboarding with certified deletion under the disposal standard |
| Customer-side party | Assessment against the terms of service and the shared-responsibility boundary | Notification, required remediation, suspension of the affected integration |

Sanctions are applied **in accordance with entity policies and applicable legal and regulatory requirements**, are
proportionate to the conduct, and are recorded. The evaluation is performed for every such incident, and the
outcome — including a determination that no sanction is warranted, with its reasoning — is recorded in the
incident record. Consistency of application is reviewed annually by Human Resources with the CISO, because
selectively applied sanctions are not a control.

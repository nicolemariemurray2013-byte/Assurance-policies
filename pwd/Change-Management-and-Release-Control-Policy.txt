# Change Management and Release Control Policy

**Document ID:** ENG-CHG-028
**Version:** 6.4 — **effective 2026-08-14**, superseding v6.3 of 2026-03-30
**Owner:** Head of Engineering (process); Head of Platform Engineering (pipeline and infrastructure changes)
**Approved by:** CISO 2026-08-14; Head of Engineering 2026-08-14
**Next scheduled review:** Annually (next by 2026-12-31), and on any change to the pipeline, the deployment model, or
the approval structure

**v6.4 changes.** Added §7.4 (development tools, compilers, editors and debuggers absent from production, and how
that is enforced by identity and immutability rather than by hygiene), §7.5 (environment identification presented
at every point a person can act, with the account boundary and two-person rule limiting what a wrong-environment
error can do), and §7.6 (patching, secure-configuration baselines, monitoring, and backup and recovery for the
development and test environments, with the proportionality decisions stated rather than left as gaps).

**Scope.** Every change to infrastructure, data, software, and manual and automated procedures that supports the
service, across the whole system lifecycle — from authorization before design through to post-implementation
verification. Applies to first-party code, acquired and open-source software, configuration, schema, and the
documented procedures people follow. The reference implementation is AWS account **471885797689**, region
**us-west-2**.

Companion documents: the configuration standards and vulnerability management procedure (baselines and patch
deadlines); the malicious software standard (what may enter production, and application control); the incident
response program (emergency and incident-derived change); the business continuity plan (resilience objectives
tested during development); the privacy and confidentiality standards (data protection during development and
test).

## 1. The lifecycle process (CC8.1-R1, CC5.2-R5)

### 1.1 Scope of "change"

The process covers all four component classes named in the criteria, because a process that covers only
application code leaves the majority of the real risk unmanaged:

| Component class | Examples | Route |
|---|---|---|
| **Software** | Application code, agent prompts and graph structure, dependencies, container base images, build tooling | Pull request → automated gates → peer review → pipeline → progressive deploy |
| **Infrastructure** | Compute, network, storage, identity policies, keys, monitoring, and alarm definitions | Infrastructure-as-code pull request → the same gates and review → pipeline apply |
| **Data** | Schema changes, migrations, reference and configuration data, retention settings | Change record with a migration and rollback plan; reversibility assessed explicitly, because data changes are the least reversible class |
| **Procedures** | Runbooks, this policy and every companion standard, on-call procedures, manual control steps | Document change with a named approver; version, effective date, and superseded version recorded on the document itself |

### 1.2 The lifecycle stages, and the gate at each one

Every change passes the same ordered stages. The gate at each stage is the control; the stage without its gate is
just an activity.

| Stage | Gate | Cannot be performed by |
|---|---|---|
| **Authorize** (§2) | Recorded authorization before design, development, acquisition, or configuration begins | — |
| **Design** (§3) | Security and privacy requirements identified; architecture assessment for significant change; resilience objectives stated | — |
| **Develop / acquire** (§3) | Secure development practices; acquisition review for third-party software | — |
| **Document** (§4) | Change record complete; user-facing and operator-facing documentation updated | — |
| **Track** (§5) | Change recorded and traceable from requirement to commit to artifact to deployment | — |
| **Configure** (§6) | Configuration parameters selected against the baseline and expressed in code | — |
| **Test** (§7) | Automated gates pass; risk-appropriate testing complete; resilience tested where applicable | — |
| **Approve** (§8) | Peer review by someone **other than the author**; risk-tiered approvals obtained | The author |
| **Implement** (§9) | Deployment by the pipeline identity, progressively, with automated rollback | The author, manually |
| **Verify** | Post-implementation health, configuration compliance, and objective-achievement checks | — |

**No stage may be skipped.** The emergency path in §10 compresses the sequence and permits some stages to complete
after implementation, but it does not permit any stage to be omitted.

## 2. Authorization before design, development, or acquisition (CC8.1-R2)

Authorization precedes work, not deployment. This matters because a change that is authorized only at the point of
release has already consumed the effort and acquired the momentum that make honest rejection unlikely.

| Change class | Authorization required | Who authorizes | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| New capability or a change to the service's external behaviour | Product authorization with a stated objective, plus a security design review | Product owner and Head of Engineering; CISO where security-relevant | Before design |
| Architecture change (new component, data store, trust boundary, external integration, region, or account) | **Pre-implementation architecture assessment** — the six required review contents in the logical access standard §3.6 | Head of Platform Engineering and CISO | Before design |
| Change to a control, an entitlement model, or an audit or encryption setting | Explicit control-change authorization | CISO | Before design |
| Acquisition of third-party or open-source software or a service | **Acquisition review**: license, security posture, data handling, sub-processor status, supplier viability, and exit path | Head of Engineering; CISO for security relevance; Privacy lead where personal information is processed; Procurement for contract | Before acquisition or first use |
| Schema or data-model change | Data change authorization with migration and rollback plan | Service owner and Head of Platform Engineering | Before development |
| Routine maintenance within an established pattern — dependency upgrade, patch, refactor, bug fix, alarm threshold | Standing authorization under this policy; the peer review at §8 is the authorization | Peer reviewer | At review |
| Emergency change | Concurrence of a second authorized person at the time (§10) | Incident commander or on-call lead plus one other | At the time |

**Standing authorization is bounded, not open-ended.** A change qualifies as routine only if it stays within an
established pattern and touches no control, trust boundary, or data model. A change that starts routine and turns
out to touch one of those stops and obtains the authorization above — the reviewer is explicitly responsible for
making that call, and "it was already in flight" is not a reason to proceed.

## 3. Secure design and development (CC8.1-R3, CC8.1-R10, CC8.1-R15 to CC8.1-R18)

### 3.1 Designing securely

- **Threat modelling** for new components and for any change to a trust boundary, data flow, or authentication
  path. The model names the assets, the boundaries, the adversaries, and the mitigations, and its unresolved items
  become work.
- **Security requirements are stated as acceptance criteria**, not as advice, so that "done" includes them.
- **Least privilege by construction**: a new component's identity is scoped to the calls it makes, and the scope is
  reviewed as part of the change rather than granted broadly and narrowed later.
- **Secure defaults**: encryption on, logging on, public access blocked, TLS required — the baselines in the
  configuration standards procedure are the starting point of a new resource definition, not a subsequent
  hardening pass.
- **Secure coding practices**: input validated at the boundary against an explicit schema; output encoded; no
  string-built queries; no dynamic evaluation of external input; dependencies from the curated repository. The
  service's design deliberately has **no code path that interprets customer-supplied content as code**.
- **Peer review is a design control**, not only a defect-detection control: the reviewer is asked whether the
  design is right, not only whether the diff is correct.

### 3.2 Identifying affected objectives and evaluating the modified system (CC8.1-R10)

Every change record names **which entity objectives and commitments it touches** — security, availability,
processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy, or a stated customer commitment — and how the modified system will
still meet them. This is evaluated at each stage, not once: at authorization (does the objective allow this?), at
design (does the design preserve it?), at test (does the evidence show it?), and at post-implementation
verification (does the running system demonstrate it?). A change that would degrade a commitment requires the
commitment to be renegotiated first, or the change to be redesigned. It is not permitted to proceed on the
assumption that the degradation will go unnoticed.

### 3.3 Resilience in design and testing (CC8.1-R15)

Resilience is a design input with stated objectives, and it is tested during development rather than assumed:

| Design property | How it is built | How it is tested before release |
|---|---|---|
| No single point of failure in the request path | Managed, multi-zone services; stateless runtime | Component-failure injection in pre-production |
| Tolerance of dependency failure | Timeouts, bounded retries with backoff and jitter, circuit breaking, and defined degraded behaviour | Dependency fault injection; latency and error injection |
| Tolerance of partial failure in a long-running assessment | Per-phase idempotency, a process-global lock with heartbeat so a dead worker's lock expires rather than blocking forever, and resumable phase state | Deliberate worker termination mid-assessment; lock expiry verification |
| Recoverability of data | Point-in-time recovery and versioned object storage | Restore rehearsal against the recovery point and time objectives |
| Capacity headroom and backpressure | Quotas, throttling, queue-depth-driven backpressure | Load and soak testing to the stated capacity objective |
| Graceful degradation over hard failure | Defined reduced-function modes | Exercised in pre-production and in the continuity tests |

Resilience test results are part of the change evidence for any change that alters the request path, a dependency,
or the state model. Where a test shows an objective is not met, the release does not proceed on the strength of an
intention to fix it later.

### 3.4 Protecting confidential and personal information during development, testing, and change (CC8.1-R16, CC8.1-R17, CC8.1-R18)

| Control | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Production data is not used in development or test** | Test data is synthetic or de-identified. Copying production personal or confidential data into a lower environment requires the Privacy lead's and the data owner's approval, is time-bounded, is recorded, and has never been the default path |
| Lower environments are separated | Distinct accounts and credentials; no network or identity path from a lower environment into production data |
| Access during development follows the same model | Least privilege, short-lived credentials, no shared accounts — a developer does not gain standing production data access by virtue of working on the code |
| Secrets never enter source | Secret scanning on every commit and over history; secrets injected at runtime from managed storage |
| Confidentiality in the pipeline | Build logs and artifacts treated as internal; log redaction of credentials and sensitive fields; artifact repository access restricted to the pipeline identity |
| **Privacy by design (CC8.1-R18)** | A privacy review is required for any change that introduces or alters the processing of personal information. It must establish the **identified purpose**, and confirm that the collection and processing are **limited to what is necessary for that purpose** — data minimisation is an acceptance criterion, so a field that no purpose requires is not added. Retention, lawful basis, data-subject-rights impact, and cross-border transfer are assessed in the same review |
| Data protection impact assessment | Required where processing is high-risk; owned by the Privacy lead; completed before development, not before launch |
| Confidentiality in test evidence | Test artifacts containing any real content are held under the same classification and retention as production evidence and disposed of under the disposal standard |

## 4. Documenting changes (CC8.1-R4)

Every change carries a record containing: a unique identifier; description and business purpose; the component
classes affected; the objectives and commitments touched (§3.2); risk classification; the security, privacy, and
resilience assessments where applicable; test evidence; the configuration parameters changed; the approvals with
approver identity and timestamp; the deployment plan; the **rollback plan**; the post-implementation verification
result; and links to the commits and the artifact digest.

The record supports **ongoing maintenance and the responsibilities of internal and external users**, which means
documentation is not only the internal record:

| Documentation | Updated when | Audience |
|---|---|---|
| Change record | Per change | Auditors, engineers, incident responders reconstructing history |
| Architecture and data-flow documentation | On any architecture or data-flow change | Engineers, security, and the information-requirements standard's flow inventory |
| Runbooks and on-call procedures | On any change to operational behaviour, alarms, or failure modes | On-call engineers — a change that alters failure behaviour without updating the runbook is incomplete and is rejected at review |
| Customer-facing documentation and release notes | On any change to external behaviour, API, or commitments | Customers, so that external users can perform their responsibilities under the shared-responsibility model |
| Control documentation (this policy and its companions) | On any change to a control | Auditors, the workforce |
| Configuration baseline | On any baseline change | Platform Engineering, and the continuous evaluation rule set |

**Documentation currency is enforced at review**, not audited afterwards: the reviewer is required to confirm that
the affected documentation in the table above has been updated in the same change. This is the control that
prevents documentation debt, and it is why the review checklist contains it explicitly.

## 5. Tracking changes prior to implementation (CC8.1-R5)

Every change is traceable end to end **before** it reaches production: requirement or incident → change record →
commit (signed, on a protected branch) → build with a recorded artifact digest → approval → deployment event. The
chain is machine-recorded, not assembled by hand at audit time.

Tracking mechanisms: a single change register as the system of record; branch protection so no commit reaches the
mainline without a tracked, reviewed pull request; commit signature verification; the pipeline recording the
digest it built and the change it built it from; and the deployment event recording the digest deployed. **The
reconciliation control** runs weekly: every production deployment is matched to an approved change record, and
every approved change is matched to a deployment or an explicit withdrawal. An unmatched deployment is treated as
unauthorized change and handled as an incident; an unmatched approval is a process defect. This reconciliation is
what makes the tracking control detective as well as preventive.

## 6. Configuration parameters of developed and acquired software (CC8.1-R6, CC8.1-R12, CC5.2-R2)

### 6.1 Selecting, implementing, maintaining, and monitoring parameters

| Activity | How |
|---|---|
| **Select** | Parameters are chosen against the hardening baselines in the configuration standards procedure and against vendor guidance; a security-relevant parameter's value requires a stated rationale, not a default accepted by inertia |
| **Implement** | Every parameter is expressed in infrastructure-as-code or in versioned application configuration. **No security-relevant parameter is set by console action** in the normal course; a console change during an incident is reconciled back into code afterwards |
| **Maintain** | Parameter changes go through this policy like any other change; vendor guidance updates are picked up at the annual baseline review and on advisory |
| **Monitor** | **368 configuration rules** evaluate the resulting state continuously, and drift reconciliation compares live state against the code. A parameter that has drifted produces a finding with an owner |

### 6.2 Baseline configuration of IT and control systems (CC8.1-R12)

The baseline is maintained as the three agreeing artifacts described in the configuration standards procedure —
the infrastructure-as-code definition, the machine-checkable evaluation rule, and the documented rationale. It is
**versioned**, so the configuration of the system at any past date is reconstructable; it covers the control
systems as well as the service (the trail, the alarms, the evaluation rule set, and the detection estate are
themselves baselined, which is why a change to them raises `AIGov-AuditConfigChanges`); and it is **re-baselined
by change record**, never by observation of what the environment happens to contain. Deviation from the baseline
is a finding, not a new baseline.

## 7. Testing prior to implementation (CC8.1-R7)

### 7.1 Automated gates on every change

These run on every change and **block** rather than warn. They are not bypassable by the author:

type checking; unit tests with a coverage floor; integration tests; lint and formatting; dependency vulnerability
scanning; license compliance; software composition analysis; secret scanning; static application security testing;
container image scanning; infrastructure-as-code policy scanning; and a build that must succeed reproducibly from
the committed lockfile.

### 7.2 Risk-tiered testing beyond the gates

| Risk tier | Additional testing |
|---|---|
| Low (isolated, reversible, no control or data-model impact) | Gates only |
| Medium (behavioural change, new dependency, alarm or entitlement change) | Pre-production functional verification; targeted regression; rollback rehearsed |
| High (architecture, data model, control, authentication, or external-behaviour change) | Full pre-production exercise with production-like data volumes; resilience and fault injection per §3.3; security testing of the changed surface; migration and rollback rehearsal; performance verification against the capacity objective |
| Data migration, any tier | Rehearsal on a restored copy; row-count and integrity reconciliation; a tested rollback or a forward-fix plan where rollback is impossible, with the irreversibility stated explicitly in the record |

**Acquired software is tested too (CC8.1-R7).** A third-party component, a vendor update, and a dependency upgrade
pass the same gates and the same risk-tiered testing as first-party code. Vendor assurance is not a substitute for
the entity's own verification: the entity's obligation is to its own objectives.

### 7.3 Testing is performed outside production

Tests run in pre-production environments that are separate accounts with their own credentials and no path to
production data. Production verification is limited to post-deployment health and integrity checks and to
progressive-exposure monitoring — not to functional discovery.

### 7.4 Development tools, compilers, and editors are absent from production

Separation is not only about data and credentials: the **tools of development are not present on operational
systems**, because a compiler or an interactive editor on a production host converts a foothold into the ability
to build and run new code there.

| Class of tool | Rule in the production runtime | How the rule is enforced, rather than asserted |
|---|---|---|
| Compilers, build toolchains, package managers, language SDKs | **Absent.** Compilation happens only in the isolated build environment; the artifact deployed is already built | The runtime image is built from a minimal base and installs no toolchain; the image build fails its policy check if a toolchain package is present. Composition analysis lists the installed package set of every released image, and the weekly reconciliation compares it against the approved manifest |
| Interactive editors (`vi`, `nano`, IDEs) and shells | **Absent, and unusable if present.** There is **no interactive access path into production at all** — no SSH, no RDP, no exec-into-container permission held by any human or role | Enforced by identity, not by hygiene: no principal, including break-glass, holds an exec or session-start permission on the runtime. The image ships no shell. An attempt to obtain one raises an alarm |
| Source code and repositories | The deployed artifact contains only what the build emits; no working copy, no version-control client, no repository credentials | The artifact is content-addressed and its contents are the build output; the runtime holds no version-control credential, so a repository could not be reached from it |
| Debuggers, profilers, remote-debug agents, diagnostic consoles | **Not enabled in production.** Diagnosis is by telemetry — logs, metrics, traces — collected outward, not by attaching to a running process | Debug ports are not opened (zero inbound security-group rules), and debug flags are asserted off by the configuration rules; a debug-enabled deployment fails its gate |
| Administrative and utility software capable of bypassing controls | Governed by the malicious software standard §3: an enumerated list, role-restricted, time-bound, and logged | Application control in enforce mode; alarms on use |
| Test data generators, load tools, migration scripts | Run from outside production against approved endpoints under change control; not installed on production hosts | Deployed as separate short-lived jobs in their own account where possible; a production-resident tool would appear in the image package set and fail reconciliation |

Two properties make the rule durable rather than aspirational: the runtime filesystem is **read-only or
ephemeral**, so a tool cannot be installed and persist even by an actor who reached the host, and every task is
**replaced from an approved digest** rather than repaired, so any local addition is destroyed on the next
deployment or scale event. **Development tooling on engineer endpoints** is a separate, permitted case: endpoints
are C-2 environments, hold no production credentials at rest, and reach production only through the pipeline and
through time-limited federated roles that carry no interactive access.

Conversely, **production tooling is not present in development**: production credentials, customer KMS keys and
customer data access are unavailable in lower environments by key policy and by the absence of any cross-account
trust, so a developer cannot reach production from a development environment either. Separation is enforced in
both directions.

In this period the weekly image-package reconciliation ran **41** times with **0** unapproved packages found, and
**0** interactive production sessions occurred (the count is derived from CloudTrail, not from self-report).

### 7.5 Environment identification, so an operator can see where they are

Acting in the wrong environment is an ordinary human error with a large consequence, so environment identity is
made visible at every point where a person could act, rather than left to be inferred from an account number.

| Where a person acts | Identification presented |
|---|---|
| AWS console | Each account carries a distinct **account alias** (`truenorth-prod`, `truenorth-staging`, `truenorth-dev`, `truenorth-audit`) and a configured console display name; the production account additionally uses a **red console banner** carrying the text `PRODUCTION — CUSTOMER DATA` on every page, set as an account-level setting so no user can turn it off for themselves |
| Command line | Named profiles matching the aliases, and a shell prompt integration on the managed build that displays the active profile and colours the prompt **red for production**; a profile that cannot be identified displays as `UNKNOWN` in red rather than as nothing |
| Internal service and admin interfaces | A persistent environment ribbon in the header — colour, environment name and region — rendered from the runtime's own configuration, so a page cannot claim to be staging while running in production |
| Deployment tooling and pipeline | The stage name in the approval prompt, and a production deployment requires an **explicit typed confirmation of the environment name**, not a default-yes prompt |
| Dashboards, alarms and logs | Environment name in the dashboard title, the alarm name prefix and every log record's structured fields, so a screenshot taken from a dashboard is self-identifying |
| Databases and data stores | Resource names carry the environment; the production data stores are in a different account, so a connection string cannot be edited from staging to production without a credential that does not exist |
| Change and incident records | The affected environment is a required field, and a record without it fails validation |

The colour convention is fixed and taught in onboarding (**red = production, amber = staging, green =
development**), and it is applied consistently so that the colour carries information. Two supporting properties
matter more than the labelling itself: the **account boundary** means a mistaken action in the wrong window most
often fails on permissions rather than succeeding on the wrong data, and **destructive production operations
require a second person**, so a single misdirected action is not sufficient. Labelling reduces the error rate; the
boundary limits what an error can do. In this period **0** wrong-environment actions were recorded; the
convention's application was verified in the annual environment review.

### 7.6 Development and test environments are themselves maintained

Lower environments are not exempt from operational discipline. They are a route into the supply chain and they
hold the entity's intellectual property, so they are patched, baselined and recoverable — proportionately, not
identically.

| Obligation | Development and CI | Pre-production / staging | Difference from production, and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Patching of the environment and its tooling** | Build images, CI runners and development tooling are rebuilt from the current base image at least **monthly** and on any critical or KEV-listed advisory affecting them; runners are ephemeral, so a rebuild fully replaces them. Engineer endpoint tooling patches on the endpoint standard's clock | Rebuilt from the **same** base images and the same infrastructure-as-code as production, so it patches when production does | Same clocks for critical and KEV items; medium and low findings may run to the routine monthly refresh, because no customer data is reachable. The deferral is a stated decision, not an omission |
| **Secure configuration baseline** | The same infrastructure-as-code modules and the same **368 configuration rules** are evaluated in the development and staging accounts, with the customer-data-specific rules not applicable | As development, plus production-equivalent network and identity rules so that staging is a valid test of the production configuration | Rules are shared, so a baseline change reaches all environments at once and staging genuinely predicts production. Findings in lower environments run on the 30-day clock rather than 7 |
| **Access control** | Role-based, MFA-required, no standing admin, reviewed in the same weekly access review | As development | Same mechanism; the population is broader because developers have more latitude in an environment holding no customer data |
| **Monitoring and logging** | CloudTrail enabled and delivered to the same central, write-once audit account; GuardDuty enabled; drift detection on | As development, plus the production alarm set so alarm behaviour is exercised before release | Lower environments do not carry 24×7 paging; findings are triaged next business day |
| **Backup and recovery** | Source code, infrastructure-as-code and pipeline definitions are the assets that matter and are backed up as version-control repositories with **daily off-account backups, 90-day retention**, plus the registry's artifact retention (§5.3 of the malware standard). CI runners and scratch data are deliberately **not** backed up — they are reproducible from code, and reproducibility is the stronger property | Rebuildable from infrastructure-as-code within the stated 4-hour target; synthetic datasets regenerated from their generators, which are themselves in version control | Production carries point-in-time recovery and cross-account immutable backups because customer data is not reproducible. Lower environments are recovered by **rebuild**, and this is a decision recorded with its rationale, not an absence of a control |
| **Recovery testing** | The rebuild path is exercised whenever a runner image or module changes, which is continuous in practice; a **quarterly** documented exercise restores a repository from off-account backup and rebuilds a runner from scratch | Rebuilt from scratch **at least quarterly** as part of the resilience exercise, which doubles as the test that staging matches production | Production recovery testing is semi-annual and formal per the continuity plan; lower-environment testing is more frequent and less formal because it is cheap |
| **Data in the environment** | Synthetic or de-identified only; copying customer data into a lower environment requires Privacy lead and data owner approval, is time-bounded and recorded (§3.4) | As development | Unchanged: this is the one obligation with no proportionality reduction |

Ownership sits with Platform Engineering, with the same deficiency register and the same quarterly Audit
Committee reporting as production. In this period: **12** runner-image rebuilds, **0** critical findings open past
their clock in any lower environment, **1** quarterly repository-restore exercise completed 2026-07-08 (restore
verified, rebuild successful, no corrective action), and staging rebuilt from scratch **2** times.

## 8. Approval and segregation of responsibilities (CC8.1-R8, CC8.1-R9, CC5.2-R1, CC5.2-R3)

### 8.1 Approval

| Change | Required approvals |
|---|---|
| Any change to the mainline | **Peer review and approval by someone other than the author**, enforced by branch protection — this is a platform-enforced control, not a convention |
| Infrastructure change | Peer review plus Platform Engineering approval |
| Control, entitlement, audit, or encryption change | Peer review plus **CISO approval** |
| Data model or migration | Peer review plus service owner approval |
| Privacy-relevant change | Peer review plus **Privacy lead approval** |
| Third-party software introduction | Peer review plus acquisition review sign-off |
| Emergency change | Second person's concurrence at the time, ratified in the weekly review (§10) |
| Procedure or policy document | Named approver per the document's governance table |

Approval is recorded with identity and timestamp, and **approval is on the reviewed content**: a change amended
after approval requires re-approval, which the platform enforces by dismissing stale approvals on new commits.

### 8.2 Segregation of responsibilities to prevent or detect unauthorized change (CC8.1-R9)

| Separation | Enforcement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Author ≠ approver** | Branch protection rejects self-approval | The most basic guard against unilateral change |
| **Author ≠ deployer** | The pipeline identity deploys; no human identity holds standing deploy permission | A person who could both write and deploy could bypass review entirely |
| **Developer ≠ production data operator** | Production data access is a separate, brokered, logged entitlement, not a property of write access to the code | Prevents a code change being used as a route to data |
| **Requester ≠ entitlement granter** | Access changes are authorized by the asset owner, not the requester | Logical access standard §5.1 |
| **Change implementer ≠ control monitor** | Security Engineering reviews findings and reconciliations, including those arising from its own changes' effects, but does not approve its own control changes — the CISO does | Prevents self-certification |
| **Pipeline gate configuration ≠ author** | Gate definitions are themselves protected code requiring review; an author cannot weaken the gate their change must pass | Otherwise every other gate is advisory |

**Where segregation is not achievable by headcount**, detective compensation is applied and stated: in a small
team the same individual may be the only qualified reviewer for a specialised change. In that case the change
requires the CISO's or Head of Engineering's concurrence as second party, and the weekly reconciliation
specifically examines changes where the reviewer pool was one. The compensating control is named rather than the
separation being quietly assumed.

### 8.3 Dependency between business processes, automated controls, and technology general controls (CC5.2-R1)

Management maintains an explicit map, reviewed annually, linking each business process (assessment execution, evidence
handling, result delivery, customer onboarding, access administration) to the automated control activities it
relies on (tenant scoping, credential vending and expiry, the assessment lock, schema validation, integrity
checks, retention enforcement) and to the technology general controls those automated controls depend on (change
management, logical access, configuration and baseline management, monitoring and incident response, backup and
recovery).

The map's purpose is decision-making, not documentation: it is what determines that a weakness in a general
control is a weakness in every automated control that rests on it, and it is consulted when scoping a change, an
incident's impact, or a control test. Its practical consequence is stated plainly — **an automated control cannot
be relied upon more strongly than the change and access controls over the system that implements it**, which is
why this policy exists and why its gates are platform-enforced rather than procedural.

## 9. Implementation (CC8.1-R1, CC6.8-R5)

Deployment is performed by the pipeline identity from a recorded artifact digest, progressively, with health and
error-rate checks between stages and **automatic rollback on regression**. Deployments are reversible by design:
either the previous digest can be redeployed, or, where a data migration makes that impossible, the
irreversibility is stated in the change record and a forward-fix plan is approved before implementation.

Post-implementation verification confirms: health and error rates within thresholds; the running digest matches
the approved release; the configuration rules evaluate compliant for the affected resources, allowing for the
up-to-24-hour periodic evaluation latency documented in the configuration standards procedure; the objectives
named in §3.2 are still met; and the documentation in §4 is current. Verification failure triggers rollback, not
investigation-in-place.

## 10. Emergency and incident-derived change (CC8.1-R13, CC8.1-R11, CC8.1-R14)

### 10.1 Emergency changes

An emergency change is one where the delay of the normal sequence would itself cause material harm — an active
incident, an exploited vulnerability, or an imminent availability loss. The compressed path:

1. **Declared** as an emergency by the incident commander or the on-call lead, with the reason recorded at the
   time.
2. **A second authorized person concurs at the time.** This is not waivable — it is the one gate the emergency
   path never compresses, because unilateral production change under time pressure is precisely the condition in
   which errors and abuse are most likely.
3. Automated gates still run. Where a gate genuinely cannot complete in the available time, the specific gate
   skipped is named in the record and re-run immediately afterwards.
4. Implemented through the pipeline where at all possible; a manual action is recorded keystroke-level in the
   incident timeline and reconciled back into infrastructure-as-code within **one working day**.
5. **Full documentation within one working day**, including everything §4 requires.
6. **Ratified in the weekly change review**, which examines every emergency change of the period: was the
   emergency real, was the compressed path justified, and has the record been completed. A pattern of emergencies
   is treated as a process defect and investigated as such — a standing high rate of emergency change means the
   normal path is too slow, and the answer is to fix the normal path, not to normalise the exception.

### 10.2 Changes required to remediate incidents (CC8.1-R11)

Identification of a needed change during an incident **initiates the change process immediately**, at the moment of
identification rather than at the end of the incident. Incident-derived changes are tagged as such, so the weekly
review examines them as a set and the quarterly incident-pattern review can see whether remediations are actually
landing. A corrective action from a post-incident review that requires a change enters the register with an owner
and a due date and is tracked to closure; an overdue corrective action escalates to the CISO.

### 10.3 Patching (CC8.1-R14)

Patches are identified from the intelligence sources in the monitoring procedure, evaluated for applicability
against the retained per-release bill of materials, tested through the standard gates, approved by peer review,
and implemented through the pipeline — to the deadlines in the vulnerability management procedure (**7 days
critical or known-exploited, 30 days high, 90 days medium, 180 days low**). Because the runtime is immutable,
patching production **is** deploying a new artifact: the same tested, approved, reversible path, which is why the
deadlines are achievable rather than aspirational. Endpoint patching runs on a monthly cadence with an emergency
path for critical items. Patches that cannot meet a deadline follow the exception process, with compensating
controls, a named owner, a dated plan, and CISO approval; exceptions expire rather than persisting.

## 11. Governance

| Activity | Frequency | Owner | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change review — emergency changes, incident-derived changes, single-reviewer changes | Weekly | Head of Engineering | Review record with dispositions |
| Deployment-to-change-record reconciliation | Weekly | Engineering management | Reconciliation with exceptions |
| Drift reconciliation against the baseline | Weekly | Platform Engineering | Drift report |
| Failed-change and rollback analysis | Monthly | Head of Engineering | Analysis with corrective actions |
| Patch currency and SLA compliance | Monthly | Security Engineering | Backlog with ages |
| Emergency-change rate trend | Quarterly | Head of Engineering | Trend with process findings |
| Approval and segregation compliance sampling | Quarterly | Internal control function | Sample results |
| Business-process to automated-control to general-control map review | Annually | Head of Engineering with CISO | Reviewed map |
| Baseline review against upstream guidance | Annually | Platform Engineering | Approved baseline diff |
| Policy review and approval | Annually | CISO and Head of Engineering | Approved version |

Deficiencies are recorded in the control deficiency register with an owner and due date, tracked to closure, and
reported to the Audit Committee quarterly.

# Cryptographic Standard and Key Management Procedure

**Document ID:** SEC-CRY-022
**Version:** 3.2 — **effective 2026-08-19**, superseding v3.1 of 2026-02-16 and v2.6 of 2025-08-04

**Revision note.** Version 3.2 adds §4A (what pervasive encryption costs the controls that inspect
content, control by control, with the compensating control named and the residual risk registered —
including the deliberate non-deployment of inline TLS interception and its reasoning), §4B (the legal
and regulatory frameworks that constrain cryptography here, export-control determinations including
the release-blocking gate that would apply if distribution were ever introduced, and the restrictions
on use — most importantly that encrypting personal data does not remove it from transfer rules and
that a key under legal hold cannot be scheduled for deletion), and §4C (the service terms of the one
cryptographic module production depends on: availability commitment, the limits of its remedy,
support response, liability, and the audit-rights deviation, each tied to why it matters to this
standard). The three additions exist because a standard that names an algorithm table but not the
detections encryption blinds, the law that constrains it, or the contract underneath it is only a
third of a standard.

**Owner:** Principal Security Engineer, Cryptography
**Approved by:** CISO 2026-02-12; v3.2 approved by the CISO 2026-08-19, with §4B reviewed by the General Counsel and §4C by the Head of Procurement
**Next scheduled review:** Annually (next by 2026-12-31), and immediately upon any cryptanalytic development, any
change in the approved-algorithm guidance the entity follows, or any key compromise

**Scope.** All use of cryptography by the service: data at rest, data in transit, data in use, credential and
token protection, signing and verification, and the full lifecycle of every key and secret. Applies to the
service's own AWS accounts, its source and build systems, its vendor integrations, and to employee endpoints.
The reference implementation is AWS account **471885797689**, region **us-west-2**.

Companion documents: the logical access standard (who may use a key), the asset inventory and classification
standard (which data classes require which protection), the media and disposal standard (cryptographic erasure
as a disposal method), and the backup standard (protection of encrypted copies).

## 1. Purpose and the risk basis for using cryptography

Cryptography is applied where the risk assessment concludes that access control alone is insufficient — that
is, wherever data could be read by a party who obtains the storage medium, intercepts the channel, or acquires
a credential without the corresponding key. The risk assessment records, for each asset class in the inventory,
whether encryption is required at rest, in transit, and in use, and that determination is what this standard
implements. Cryptography is not applied decoratively: each use below traces to a threat the risk assessment
identified.

| Threat identified by the risk assessment | Cryptographic control selected | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure of stored customer evidence or assessment results if storage is obtained | Encryption at rest with KMS-held keys | Mandatory for Restricted and Confidential |
| Interception of credentials or data on a network path | TLS 1.2+, enforced by resource policy | Mandatory on every channel |
| Replay or forgery of an agent invocation | Asymmetric signature over a KMS-held private key | Mandatory |
| Reuse of an intercepted request signature | Per-request SigV4 signing with short-lived credentials | Mandatory |
| Recovery of data from a disposed medium | Cryptographic erasure via key destruction | Mandatory (see the disposal standard) |
| Tampering with a build artifact between build and deploy | Artifact digest verification and signed provenance | Mandatory |
| Tampering with an audit record | Log file validation digests on the audit trail | Mandatory |

## 2. Approved cryptographic modules, algorithms, key lengths, and architectures (CC6.1-R15)

### 2.1 Approved algorithms

| Purpose | Approved | Minimum key length / parameters | Prohibited |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetric encryption at rest | AES-GCM (preferred), AES-CBC with a separate MAC | **AES-256** | DES, 3DES, RC4, Blowfish, AES-128 for new use |
| Asymmetric encryption | RSA-OAEP, ECIES over P-256 or higher | **RSA-3072** for new keys; RSA-2048 permitted only for existing keys with a migration date | RSA < 2048, any use of PKCS#1 v1.5 encryption |
| Digital signature | ECDSA over P-256/P-384, RSASSA-PSS, Ed25519 | **P-256** / **RSA-2048 with PSS** minimum; RS256 accepted where the verifying platform requires it | DSA, RSA with MD5 or SHA-1 |
| Hashing | SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512 | 256-bit minimum | MD5, SHA-1 (except where required for a non-security checksum, which must be labelled as such) |
| Message authentication | HMAC-SHA-256 or stronger, AES-GCM tags | 256-bit key | HMAC-MD5, HMAC-SHA1 |
| Key derivation | HKDF-SHA-256, PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256 with ≥ 600,000 iterations, scrypt, Argon2id | Per algorithm | Single-round hashing of a secret |
| Password storage (where unavoidable) | Argon2id, or bcrypt cost ≥ 12 | Per algorithm | Any reversible encoding; any unsalted hash |
| Transport | TLS 1.2 and TLS 1.3, AEAD suites with forward secrecy | ECDHE key exchange; P-256 or X25519 | TLS 1.0/1.1, SSL, static-RSA key exchange, renegotiation |
| Randomness | Platform CSPRNG (`getrandom`, `crypto.randomBytes`), hardware RNG in the key store | 128 bits of entropy minimum for a token, 256 for a key | Any non-cryptographic PRNG, any time-seeded source |

Deprecation is scheduled, not reactive: each row carries a review date, and an algorithm moved to the prohibited
column gets a migration plan with an owner before the move takes effect. Certificate lifetimes are capped at 13
months and are automated so that no renewal depends on a person remembering.

### 2.2 Approved cryptographic modules

Cryptography is performed by **validated, hardware-backed modules**, not by application code:

| Module | Validation basis | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| AWS KMS | FIPS 140 validated HSMs; keys never leave the module in plaintext | All key storage, symmetric data-key wrapping, signing operations |
| Managed service envelope encryption (S3, DynamoDB, EBS, logs) | Performed inside the provider's validated modules using KMS-held keys | Encryption at rest |
| Managed TLS termination | Provider's validated TLS stack; FIPS endpoints available and used where required | Transport |
| Platform runtime crypto library (OpenSSL-backed, via the Node runtime) | Provider-maintained, patched through the base image pipeline | Client-side TLS, digest computation |

**Application code does not implement cryptographic primitives.** It calls the module. Writing a bespoke
cipher, mode, padding scheme, or key-derivation routine is prohibited without CISO approval, on the reasoning
that novel cryptographic code is the single most reliable source of cryptographic failure.

### 2.3 Approved architectures

- **Envelope encryption** everywhere at rest: a per-object or per-table data key encrypted under a KMS key. This
  makes key rotation cheap and key destruction decisive.
- **Separation of the key from the data**: the key store is a distinct service with its own authorization plane,
  so obtaining storage access does not obtain decryption capability.
- **Asymmetric where the verifier and signer are different trust domains**: the agent session token is signed
  with a private key that never leaves KMS and verified with the public key, so the verifier holds nothing that
  would let it forge a token.
- **Per-request signing rather than bearer secrets** for programmatic API access.
- **Encryption context / additional authenticated data** bound to the tenant and purpose on every encrypt
  operation, so a ciphertext cannot be replayed into a different tenant's context.

The architecture choices are recorded with the risk each addresses, and reassessed annually against current
guidance.

## 3. Encryption at rest, in transit, and in use (CC6.1-R13, CC6.7-R2)

### 3.1 At rest

Every store holding Restricted or Confidential data is encrypted at rest, with the state verified continuously
rather than assumed:

| Store | Mechanism | Verified state in the reference account |
|---|---|---|
| Object storage (evidence, results, logs, artifacts) | Server-side encryption with KMS-held keys, envelope-encrypted per object | Encryption enabled on all in-scope buckets; the S3 default key has **automatic annual rotation enabled** (365-day period) |
| Document / record store | Server-side encryption with a KMS key | The `Demo` table is encrypted with **SSE type KMS** |
| Block storage | Provider-managed encryption using KMS keys | **EBS encryption-by-default is enabled** in the in-scope region (`GetEbsEncryptionByDefault` → `true`), so a volume cannot be created unencrypted; the account currently runs no instances and holds no volumes or snapshots, and **EBS snapshot public access is set to `block-all-sharing`** |
| Log and audit data | Encrypted at rest; the audit trail additionally carries log file validation digests | Enabled — the in-scope trail is encrypted with the **customer-managed key `alias/cloudtrail-rokap-pdx`**, which has **automatic rotation enabled on a 365-day period**, and carries log file validation |
| S3 server access logs and VPC flow logs | Dedicated log bucket with default encryption (AES-256 with bucket keys), versioning, a `Deny` on non-TLS transport, public access blocked on all four dimensions, and lifecycle expiry | Enabled |
| Backups and snapshots | Inherit the source's encryption; copies are re-encrypted under the destination-region key | Enabled |
| Secrets and configuration | Held in the managed secret store, encrypted under a KMS key, never in source or in an image | Enabled |
| Customer-managed key material | Never exported; `Origin: AWS_KMS`, generated inside the module | Verified |

The reference account's key inventory is small and fully enumerated in the key register (§4.6):

| Key | Manager | Rotation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| `alias/aws/s3` | Provider-managed | **Enabled, 365-day period, next rotation scheduled** | Default object-storage encryption |
| `alias/aws/dynamodb` | Provider-managed | Provider's own annual schedule | Default record-store encryption |
| `alias/cloudtrail-rokap-pdx` | **Customer-managed** | **Enabled, 365-day period** | Audit trail encryption; key policy grants `GenerateDataKey*` to CloudTrail only under an encryption-context condition naming the trail ARN, and `Decrypt` only to account principals under the same condition |
| `alias/rokap-test-eds` | Customer-managed | Not enabled — recorded as an open deficiency with a named owner and due date, because the key's own policy denies key administration to every current identity, so rotation cannot be configured without a policy change that is itself change-managed | Legacy test data set |

The last row is deliberately left in the register rather than removed: its key policy is scoped so tightly that
even an account administrator identity is **denied `kms:DescribeKey`** on it. That is simultaneously a
demonstration of the principle in §4.3 — authority over a key comes from the key policy, not from account
administrator status — and the reason its rotation setting is an open item. The register carries both facts, which
is the behaviour this procedure requires of it.

**A note on evidence latency, so that a reader can reconcile apparently conflicting evidence.** The at-rest state
above is the state of the resource configuration, read from the service APIs. The continuous rule set that
monitors it evaluates account-level settings **periodically, up to 24 hours apart**, so for a period after a
configuration is corrected the aggregated findings view still reports the prior state. Where a finding and a
direct API read disagree, the API read is the current state and the finding is the state at its last evaluation
timestamp; the reconciliation is recorded in the remediation ticket, and the finding is expected to clear at the
next evaluation without further action. This latency is a property of the monitoring, not of the control, and it
is why remediation is verified by a direct read rather than by waiting for a finding to disappear.

### 3.2 In transit (CC6.7-R2)

Protection of data beyond a connectivity access point is by encryption of the channel, enforced at the resource
rather than requested of the client:

- **TLS 1.2 minimum, TLS 1.3 preferred**, AEAD suites with forward secrecy, on every internal and external
  channel. Plaintext protocols are not offered anywhere.
- **Enforced by resource policy**: every one of the **12 in-scope buckets** carries an explicit `Deny` on
  requests where `aws:SecureTransport` is `false`. A client that attempts a cleartext request is refused by the
  service, not merely discouraged by configuration; because the denial is in the resource policy, no
  identity-based grant can override it.
- **Server authentication** on every connection: certificates validated against the trust store; certificate
  pinning where a channel's counterparty is fixed.
- **Mutual authentication or request signing** on programmatic paths, so the channel authenticates both ends.
- **Cross-account and cross-region traffic** uses the provider's TLS-protected service endpoints; no
  self-managed tunnels carry service data.
- **Egress to vendors** is over TLS to named endpoints recorded in the vendor register; a new egress
  destination requires a change and a register entry.
- **Email and file exchange** are not used to move Restricted or Confidential data. Where an external party
  requires a file, it is delivered through an authenticated, TLS-protected, expiring link, never as an
  attachment.

### 3.3 In use

- Plaintext exists only in process memory for the duration of a request, and is not written to disk, swap, or a
  temporary file. Runtime storage is ephemeral and encrypted.
- **Log redaction** removes credentials, tokens, and personal information before a record is emitted, so
  plaintext does not leak into the observability path.
- Data is decrypted only in the account and role that has a purpose for it; the read path decrypts within the
  tenant scope derived from the authenticated session.
- Crash dumps and core files are disabled in production images so that memory contents cannot be captured to
  persistent storage.

## 4. Key management: protection through generation, storage, use, and destruction (CC6.1-R14)

### 4.1 Generation

- Keys are **generated inside the key-management module** using its hardware RNG. Key material is never
  generated on a laptop, in application code, or in a script.
- Import of external key material is prohibited unless the CISO approves it for a specific interoperability
  need, in which case the import uses the module's wrapped-import mechanism and the source material is destroyed
  immediately after import, with the destruction witnessed and recorded.
- Each key is created **by the change that deploys the workload needing it**, with its purpose, owner, data
  classes, algorithm, and rotation period recorded in the key register at creation time.
- Distinct keys for distinct purposes and distinct environments. Production keys are never used in
  pre-production, and one key never serves two unrelated data sets, so that compromise or destruction is
  contained.

### 4.2 Storage

- **Key material never exists in plaintext outside the module.** It is not exportable, and the module's API
  offers no operation that returns it.
- Data keys are stored only in their **wrapped** form alongside the data they protect; the wrapping key stays in
  the module.
- **No key, secret, or credential is stored in source control, in a container image, in an environment variable
  baked at build time, in a configuration file, in a ticket, or in a chat message.** Secret scanning runs on
  every commit and on the full history; a detected secret is treated as compromised and rotated, not merely
  deleted from the diff.
- Secrets consumed at runtime are fetched from the managed secret store by the workload's role at start-up or
  on demand, and cached in memory only.
- Key policies, not just identity policies, control access; the key policy is the authoritative statement of who
  may use a key, and it names principals and actions explicitly.

### 4.3 Use

- Use of a key requires an authorization on **both** the key policy and the caller's identity policy. Authority
  over a key is not conferred by account administrator status: the customer-managed key in the reference
  account denies key operations to an administrator identity that has no purpose for it.
- **Every key use is logged** with the caller identity, the operation, and the encryption context, in the audit
  trail. Key usage is therefore attributable per request.
- **Encryption context binding**: encrypt and decrypt operations supply an encryption context naming the tenant
  and purpose; a decrypt with a mismatched context fails, which prevents a ciphertext from one tenant being read
  in another's context.
- **Separation of duties over keys**: the role that can use a key to decrypt cannot change the key policy; the
  role that administers keys cannot use them to read data. Both actions are logged, and changes to key policy
  raise a change record.
- Signing keys are used only through the module's sign operation; the private key is never handed to
  application code.

### 4.4 Rotation

| Key or secret type | Rotation | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| KMS symmetric data-protection keys | **Annually**, automatic | Provider-managed backing-key rotation; enabled with a 365-day period on the object-storage key, next rotation scheduled |
| Data keys (per object/record) | Per object or per write | Envelope encryption; no explicit action needed |
| Asymmetric signing keys | Annually, or immediately on suspicion | New key version issued, verifiers accept both during an overlap window, old version disabled then scheduled for deletion |
| TLS certificates | ≤ 13 months, automated | Managed certificate issuance and renewal |
| Service credentials and API keys | ≤ 90 days | Managed secret store rotation, or scripted rotation with a verification step |
| Break-glass access keys | Quarterly and after every use | Manual, two-person, recorded |
| Any key or secret suspected compromised | Immediately | Incident procedure: rotate, revoke old, assess exposure, record |

Rotation state is **monitored, not trusted**: key age and rotation configuration are evaluated by the
continuous configuration-rule set (368 rules active in the reference account, findings aggregated into a single
security-findings view), and a key past its rotation window raises a finding with an owner and a due date.
Rotation is designed to be non-disruptive — readers accept the previous version for a defined overlap, so
rotation is never deferred for fear of an outage.

### 4.5 Destruction

- Key destruction is a **deliberate, authorized, delayed, and logged** operation. It requires the key owner's
  request, the security owner's approval, and a confirmation that no live data still depends on the key.
- Destruction uses the module's `ScheduleKeyDeletion` with a **waiting period of at least 30 days** (7 days is
  permitted only for a key proven unused, with CISO approval). During the waiting period the key is disabled, so
  the effect is immediate while the action remains reversible.
- **Cryptographic erasure** is an approved disposal method: where the only copies of data are encrypted under a
  single key, destroying the key renders the data unrecoverable and is recorded as the disposal event for that
  data, with the key identifier, the data set, and the timestamp. This is the mechanism relied on for disposal
  of encrypted backups and for tenant offboarding.
- Deletion of a key is preceded by a check that no ciphertext requiring future access remains, and followed by
  a verification that decrypt operations against it fail.
- Secrets are destroyed in the secret store (not merely overwritten with a placeholder), and the destruction is
  recorded in the key register, which retains a closed entry rather than deleting the history.

### 4.6 The key register

Every key and long-lived secret has a register entry containing: identifier and alias; purpose; owner;
algorithm, key length, and module; data classes protected; the key policy's authorized principals; creation
date; rotation period and last rotation; environments it serves; and, when closed, the destruction date and
authorization. The register is reconciled against the actual key inventory **quarterly**, and any key present in
the environment but absent from the register is an exception investigated within two working days — an
unregistered key is treated as a potential unauthorized data path.

## 4A. What pervasive encryption costs the controls that need to read content

Encrypting everything is not free. Every control that works by *inspecting content* — malware
scanning, data-loss prevention, content filtering, intrusion detection on payloads, and log
analysis — loses its input at exactly the point the traffic or the object becomes opaque. This
section states where that happens, what was decided about it, and why, because the alternative is
that a detection control is silently blind and nobody has written down that it is.

| Inspection control | What encryption does to it | Position taken, and why |
|---|---|---|
| Malware scanning of files in transit | TLS terminates at the AWS load balancer for inbound service traffic, so inbound payloads *are* inspectable at that boundary. Outbound and third-party TLS is not decrypted | Accepted loss. We do **not** deploy inline TLS interception. An interception proxy holds the plaintext of every session including personnel authentication and third-party credentials, becomes the single highest-value target in the estate, and breaks certificate pinning in tooling we depend on. The compensating controls are endpoint-side (EDR sees the file after decryption, where the file actually executes) and egress-side (allowlisted destinations, per `SEC-NET-023`). This is the same determination recorded in `SEC-REG-047` §3.3 and it is a deliberate non-deployment, not an omission |
| DLP on outbound content | Encrypted channels and encrypted attachments defeat pattern-based egress inspection | Partially accepted. DLP operates where plaintext exists: at the endpoint agent before encryption, at the email gateway before transport encryption, and in the approved SaaS tenants via API rather than in-line. **Personnel encryption of content specifically to evade inspection is prohibited** (`SEC-REG-047` §5.2), which converts an undetectable technical bypass into a disciplinary matter with a named rule behind it |
| Content filtering / URL categorisation | Category decisions are made on SNI and DNS, not on page content | Accepted. Filtering is by destination, not by content, and that limitation is stated rather than implied |
| Network intrusion detection on payloads | Payload signatures cannot run on encrypted flows | Accepted. Detection shifts to flow metadata (VPC flow logs), to the application layer where the request is already decrypted, and to CloudTrail. `SEC-MON-026` records which detections depend on payload visibility — currently none — so that a future detection is not written against an input we do not have |
| Log and evidence analysis | Encrypted-at-rest logs are readable by the analysis role because it holds a grant on the key, so this is a key-policy question rather than a visibility one | No loss, but a dependency: if the analysis role loses its KMS grant, the analysis silently returns nothing rather than failing loudly. This is monitored as a key-policy assertion in the quarterly reconciliation |
| Backup integrity verification | Verification of an encrypted backup requires the key at verification time, which puts the restore test on the key's availability | No loss; the restore test explicitly exercises the key path, which is the point — a backup whose key is unavailable is not a backup |
| Assessment evidence held for a customer | Customer evidence encrypted under a customer-scoped key cannot be read by any internal analysis path not granted on that key | Intended. This is a confidentiality control operating as designed, and the resulting inability to inspect is a feature, recorded here so it is not later mistaken for a broken control |

**The rule this section exists to state:** where a control's input is removed by encryption, the loss
is written down, a compensating control is named, and the residual risk is accepted by the CISO in
the risk register. A blind control that is documented as sighted is worse than no control, because
the coverage map lies.

Two current entries in the risk register carry this: `R-2026-031` (no packet capture, and therefore
no payload-level forensics — also recorded in `SEC-FOR-045` §2.1) and `R-2026-034` (no outbound TLS
inspection, with the endpoint- and egress-side compensations above). Both are reviewed annually
against whether the trade-off still holds.

## 4B. Legal, regulatory and contractual constraints on cryptography

The authoritative register of legal and contractual requirements is `SEC-REG-047`; §5 of that
document holds the full analysis. This section states the determinations that bind *this* standard,
so that an engineer reading the crypto standard does not have to know the register exists to comply
with it. Where the two disagree, the register governs and this section is corrected at the next
review — the reconciliation is a standing item in the register's semi-annual review.

### 4B.1 Applicable legal and regulatory frameworks

| Framework | How it constrains cryptography here |
|---|---|
| **UK GDPR / EU GDPR (Art. 32)** | Encryption named as an appropriate technical measure; encryption at rest and in transit is therefore a compliance obligation and not an engineering preference. Art. 34 breach-notification exposure is reduced where data was encrypted and the key was not compromised, which is why key-compromise assessment is part of the incident procedure |
| **UK Data Protection Act 2018 / EU Data Act** | As above, plus the requirement that a key whose data is under statutory retention cannot be scheduled for deletion (see §4B.3) |
| **US state privacy statutes (CCPA/CPRA and successors)** | Encryption-based safe harbour for breach notification, conditional on the key not having been exposed |
| **PCI DSS** | **Not applicable** — no cardholder data is processed, stored or transmitted. Stated positively rather than omitted, so that the absence is a determination and not a gap (the completeness test in `SEC-REG-047` §2) |
| **HIPAA** | **Not applicable** — no protected health information in scope. Same reasoning |
| **FIPS 140-3** | AWS KMS operates validated HSMs; we rely on the provider's validation and do not assert our own. Where a customer contract requires a FIPS endpoint, the FIPS service endpoints are used and the requirement is recorded against that customer |
| **Computer Misuse Act 1990 / equivalent** | Constrains what we may do with keys and encrypted material belonging to others, including during a customer-environment scan |
| **Investigatory Powers Act 2016 (UK), and equivalents** | Lawful-access demands for keys or plaintext; handling is counsel-routed and we hold **no capability to defeat our own encryption on demand**, which is a design decision recorded in `SEC-REG-047` §5.3 |

### 4B.2 Export control and cross-border restrictions

| Determination | Statement |
|---|---|
| Do we develop cryptography? | No. We implement no cryptographic primitives and ship no cryptographic library. Encryption is consumed as a hosted AWS service, which places the controlled technology with the provider |
| Do we distribute software containing cryptography? | Not currently. The service is hosted, not distributed. **If distribution is ever introduced, a pre-release export classification gate applies** — this is a named release-blocking check, not an aspiration |
| Applicable regimes considered | Wassenaar Arrangement Category 5 Part 2 (dual-use cryptography); UK Strategic Export Control Lists; US EAR §742.15 and the mass-market/publicly-available exceptions; EU Regulation 2021/821 |
| Technology transfer by personnel location | Cryptographic technical knowledge moving with personnel between jurisdictions is assessed against deemed-export rules; the current personnel distribution across five geographies has been assessed with no restriction identified |
| Sanctions and restricted destinations | Service access from sanctioned jurisdictions is blocked at the edge; sanctions screening applies to customers and suppliers per `SEC-REG-047` §3.3 |
| New region or new jurisdiction | Deploying to a new AWS region, or serving a customer in a new jurisdiction, triggers a documented export and cryptography-restriction assessment **before** launch |

### 4B.3 Restrictions on the use of encryption, and trans-border flows of encrypted information

| Restriction | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Where law mandates encryption | Encryption is mandatory and cannot be waived by exception. The §5 exception process explicitly **cannot** grant relief from a legally mandated encryption control |
| Where law restricts encryption | No jurisdiction we currently operate in restricts our use of the approved algorithms. This is re-tested on every new-jurisdiction assessment rather than assumed to be permanent |
| Trans-border flow of encrypted data | **Encrypting personal data does not remove it from data-transfer rules.** Encrypted personal data remains personal data where we hold the key, so a transfer of ciphertext across a border is still a transfer requiring a lawful mechanism. Current position: all production personal data resides in `us-west-2`; EU-origin personal data transfers rely on the mechanisms recorded in `SEC-REG-047` §3.1, and encryption is a supplementary measure supporting those mechanisms, **not** a substitute for them |
| Key location relative to data location | Keys are held in the same region as the data they protect. A key held in a second jurisdiction creates an access path from that jurisdiction to the plaintext, which is a transfer consideration in its own right |
| Legal hold and statutory retention | **A key whose data is under statutory retention or legal hold cannot be scheduled for deletion.** Deleting the key destroys the data, which would be spoliation. The key register carries a legal-hold flag, and the destruction workflow refuses a flagged key |
| Personal or unmanaged encryption | Personnel may not apply their own encryption to entity information outside the approved modules — it renders data unrecoverable by the entity and defeats the inspection controls in §4A |
| Full-disk encryption and escrow | FDE is mandatory on endpoints **and** key escrow is mandatory with it. FDE without escrow is a data-destruction risk and blocks lawful evidence acquisition (`SEC-FOR-045` §5) |

## 4C. Service terms for the cryptographic module we depend on

AWS KMS is the sole approved cryptographic module for production key material, which makes its
contractual terms a security dependency rather than a procurement detail. The terms are summarised
here because a standard that names a module without naming what is owed on it leaves the
availability of every encrypted asset undocumented.

| Term | What applies | Why it matters to this standard |
|---|---|---|
| **Availability commitment** | AWS KMS Service Level Agreement: 99.999% monthly uptime commitment, with service-credit remedies at defined tiers below that | KMS is on the critical path of every read of encrypted data. Its availability target is effectively the ceiling on the availability of everything it protects |
| **Remedy and its limits** | Service credits only. **Credits do not compensate for our own availability obligations to customers** | This is the reason the continuity plan does not treat the SLA as a control: a credit does not restore service. Cross-region key availability and the documented degraded-mode behaviour in `BCP-PLN-020` are the actual mitigations |
| **Durability of key material** | AWS commits to durability of stored CMK material; key material cannot be exported, which is the property that makes the module trustworthy and also the property that removes our ability to hold an independent copy | Recorded as an accepted dependency, `R-2026-035`, with the compensating design being multi-region keys for assets whose loss would be unrecoverable |
| **Support response times** | AWS Enterprise Support: 15-minute response target for business-critical system-down cases, 24×7, with a designated technical account manager | A key-availability event is a business-critical case. The incident runbook names this severity and the escalation path explicitly, so the response target is usable rather than theoretical |
| **Liability** | Governed by the AWS Customer Agreement, including its limitation of liability. Liability for a key-availability or key-loss event is **capped and does not approach the business impact** | Stated plainly because it is the basis for treating key loss as a risk we carry ourselves. The register entry is `R-2026-035` and the insurance position is in `SEC-REG-047` §6.3 |
| **Security and compliance posture** | AWS SOC 1/2/3, ISO 27001, and FIPS 140-3 validation of the HSMs, obtained annually through AWS Artifact | This is how the provider-operated part of our cryptographic control is assured; the reports are read, not merely collected, and the CUECs relevant to KMS are mapped in `GOV-PRO-007` §5 |
| **Change and deprecation notice** | Contractual and published deprecation timelines for API and algorithm support | Feeds the annual approved-algorithm review in §6, so an upstream deprecation reaches this standard before it reaches production |
| **Data processing terms** | AWS Data Processing Addendum, incorporated | Required for the personal-data determinations in §4B.3 |
| **Audit and evidence rights** | Third-party attestation reports rather than a right of direct inspection, which is standard for a hyperscale provider and is recorded as an accepted deviation from the general supplier audit-rights requirement | The exception is `D-2026-028` in `SEC-REG-047` §7 — the same recorded exception, not a second undocumented one |

## 5. Roles, exceptions, and enforcement

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Principal Security Engineer, Cryptography | Owns this standard, the approved-algorithm table, and the annual architecture reassessment |

| CISO | Approves exceptions, key destruction below the standard waiting period, and any bespoke cryptographic code |

**Exceptions** require a documented business reason, a compensating control, CISO approval, and an expiry no
more than 12 months out; they are held in the exception register and reviewed quarterly. Use of a prohibited
algorithm, storage of key material outside the module, or committing a secret to source control is a security
violation handled under the code of conduct and, where data was exposed, under the incident response procedure.

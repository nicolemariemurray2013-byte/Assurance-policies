**Version:** 5.1 (current) — **effective 2026-08-21**

**Scope.** Testing of the entity's ability to respond to, recover from, and resume operations through significant

the incident response and recovery program (how a disruption is run); the capacity management standard (surviving
capacity); the change management policy (the route by which test findings become changes).

## 1. Why this plan tests rather than asserts

A continuity plan that has never been executed is a hypothesis. The controlling principle of this plan is that
**every recovery claim the entity makes must have a measured result behind it**, and that a recovery capability
which has not been exercised within its test cycle is reported as *unverified* rather than as *available*.

Three consequences follow, and they shape everything below:

1. **Recovery objectives are figures that were measured, not figures that were chosen.** Where a stated objective
   has never been observed, it is labelled an assumption.
2. **A test that passes everything is treated with suspicion**, because it usually means the scenario was too
   gentle rather than that the system is perfect. Scenario difficulty is reviewed as part of the test design.
3. **A finding that produces no change is closed with a written reason.** Silence is not a disposition.

## 1A. Business impact analysis — where the objectives and the strategy come from

A recovery objective that was chosen because it sounded reasonable is not an objective, it is a
preference. Every figure in this plan traces to the **business impact analysis (BIA)** below, which is
performed annually and after any material change to the service, the customer commitments, or the
architecture. The BIA was last performed **2026-04-14**, reviewed by the Audit Committee on 2026-05-01,
and it is the document that decides three things in order: which activities matter, how long each can
be down, and — only then — what we build to meet that.

### 1A.1 Method

The BIA is run as a structured assessment with the activity owner, not as a survey. For each business
activity we establish: what the activity delivers and to whom; what depends on it; the consequence of
its interruption over time (1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day, 3 days, 1 week) across five impact categories —
**customer obligation, regulatory and legal, financial, reputational, and personnel safety**; the point
at which the consequence becomes unacceptable; and the maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD)
that follows from it. Impact is assessed **over time rather than as a single number**, because almost
every activity here is tolerable for an hour and intolerable for a week, and a single-number assessment
loses exactly the information the objective depends on.

Two rules keep the analysis honest:

- **The activity owner states the impact; the plan owner does not.** An engineer's estimate of how
  badly a customer is affected is a guess, and the guess is always lower than the answer.
- **An objective may not be tighter than the impact justifies.** Over-tight objectives are as much a
  finding as loose ones: they consume engineering capacity that a genuinely critical activity needs,
  and they train people to disbelieve the numbers.

### 1A.2 Prioritized activities and their objectives

Activities are ranked by MTPD. The priority column is the recovery order, and it is the order the
region-loss exercise in §2 actually executes in.

| Priority | Business activity | Primary impact if interrupted | MTPD | **RTO** | **RPO** | Basis for the RTO/RPO |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | **Evidence and assessment data integrity and durability** — customer compliance evidence remains intact and retrievable | Regulatory and contractual; loss is unrecoverable and undermines every downstream attestation. Impact is severe at 1 hour and irreversible thereafter | Effectively zero for *loss*; hours for *access* | **4 h** (access) | **≤ 5 min** (point-in-time recovery) | Data loss is the only impact category assessed as irreversible. The RPO is set by irreversibility, not by convenience, and drives the continuous-backup design in §6 |
| **P2** | **Customer access to completed assessment results and evidence** — the read path | Customers cannot evidence their own compliance to their auditors; contractual availability commitments engage at 4 h | 8 h | **4 h** | ≤ 5 min | Set by the customer contractual commitment plus the observed auditor-deadline pattern; measured at 2 h 41 m in the 2026-04 region exercise |
| **P3** | **Assessment execution** — the scheduled and on-demand evaluation path | Assessments queue rather than fail. Impact is low at 4 h, material at 3 days when scheduled compliance cycles start to slip | 3 days | **24 h** | ≤ 1 h | Deliberately looser than P2 because the work is resumable and idempotent — a queued assessment runs late, it does not produce a wrong answer. This is the clearest case of the analysis, not the architecture, setting the figure |
| **P4** | **Incident detection and response capability** — monitoring, alerting, audit logging | A blind period during a disruption is when a second, unrelated compromise is least likely to be seen. Regulatory notification clocks run regardless | 24 h | **8 h** | ≤ 15 min (log completeness) | Set by the shortest regulatory notification clock in `SEC-REG-047` §3, since a notification decision cannot be made without the logs |
| **P5** | **Customer and regulator communication** — the ability to tell people what is happening | Silence during a disruption converts an availability event into a trust event, and misses notification deadlines | 4 h for the *capability* | **2 h** | n/a | Tightest RTO in the set, and the cheapest to meet: contact chains, templates and an out-of-band channel that does not depend on the platform being recovered |
| **P6** | Compliance reporting and evidence export for our own audits | Internal deadlines; absorbable for a week | 2 weeks | 72 h | ≤ 24 h | Lowest priority is stated rather than omitted, so that recovery effort is not spent here first |
| **P7** | Corporate functions — mail, ticketing, documentation | Coordination friction; the offline continuity copies in `OPS-STD-049` §3 exist for exactly this | 3 days | 24 h | ≤ 4 h | Provider-dependent; the offline copies are the compensating measure |

**Dependency mapping.** Each activity's supporting resources are recorded — people (named roles and
their alternates, §5.1), technology (accounts, regions, services), data (the classes in §6), suppliers
(§5.2), and facilities. The mapping is what turns a priority into an executable order: P2 cannot be
recovered before the identity path and the key management dependency, so those are recovered inside
P2's window rather than being separate priorities competing with it.

**Resource requirements at time of recovery** are stated too — the minimum viable capacity per
priority, which is lower than steady-state capacity for P3 (a backlog can be worked down) and equal to
it for P2 (customers arrive at the same rate whether or not we are recovering).

### 1A.3 How the strategy was selected from these outputs

The continuity strategies below were **chosen against the BIA outputs**, and the reasoning is recorded
so that a future reviewer can see which figure each design decision is serving. Options were compared
on capability against the RTO/RPO, cost, and operational complexity — and complexity was treated as a
security cost, not only an engineering one, because a mechanism nobody can operate under pressure does
not deliver its stated RTO.

| BIA output driving it | Strategies considered | Selected | Why the alternatives were rejected |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 RPO ≤ 5 min, loss irreversible | Nightly snapshots; continuous point-in-time recovery; synchronous multi-region replication | **Continuous point-in-time recovery with cross-region backup copies, versioning, and restore verified quarterly** | Nightly snapshots cannot meet a 5-minute RPO. Synchronous multi-region replication meets it but was rejected: it propagates a logical corruption or malicious deletion instantly to the second copy, which is the failure mode the irreversibility finding is actually about. Point-in-time recovery is chosen *because* it retains history |
| P2 RTO 4 h | Warm standby in a second region; cold rebuild from infrastructure-as-code; accepting a longer RTO | **Multi-AZ by default, with an annually exercised cold rebuild in the alternate region from code** | Warm standby meets the RTO with more margin but doubles the estate, and the BIA does not justify it: the measured cold-rebuild time (2 h 41 m) is inside 4 h. **The decision is revisited each cycle against the measured figure, not once** — if the rebuild time drifts past 3 h, warm standby is reconsidered |
| P3 RTO 24 h, work is resumable | Queue-and-replay; synchronous failover; manual re-submission | **Queue with idempotent re-execution and lock expiry** | Synchronous failover is unjustified spend for a 24-hour objective. Manual re-submission was rejected because it depends on a person knowing what was in flight |
| P4 RTO 8 h, driven by notification clocks | Logs in the primary account only; export to a separate write-once audit account; third-party SIEM as the only copy | **Write-once audit account, cross-region, independent of the workload account** | Primary-account-only fails the scenario where the account itself is the incident. SIEM-only makes a supplier the sole custodian of the evidence needed to assess a supplier incident |
| P5 RTO 2 h, must not depend on the platform | Platform-hosted comms; out-of-band channel plus offline contact list and templates | **Out-of-band, with the four offline documents in `OPS-STD-049` §3** | A communication capability reachable only through the system being recovered is the failure the 2026-06 exercise found |
| P6/P7, low priority | — | **Accepted longer objectives, no dedicated investment** | Recorded as a deliberate decision so that "we have no strategy for this" is a choice rather than an oversight |
| Concentration on one provider (all priorities) | Multi-provider; single provider with multi-region; single provider single region | **Single provider, multi-region capability** | Assessed and accepted as `R-2026-030`, with the reasoning in `SEC-POL-051` §8. Multi-provider was rejected on the grounds that operating two estates consistently is beyond current capacity, and inconsistent operation is itself a security risk (`OPS-STD-049` §6) |

**Traceability rule.** Every objective in this plan appears in the table in §1A.2, and every strategy in
this plan appears in §1A.3 against the output that justifies it. The annual review checks the
correspondence in both directions: an objective with no BIA basis, and a strategy serving no objective,
are both findings. The 2026-04 review found one of each — the P6 objective had been carried forward
from v4.1 without a stated basis, and the communication capability had a strategy but no objective —
which is why both now appear explicitly.

**Approval.** The BIA outputs and the resulting objectives are approved by the executive team, not by
engineering, because they are statements about acceptable business consequence. Approved 2026-04-29 by
the Head of Engineering and the COO; the P1 irreversibility finding was escalated to and acknowledged
by the Audit Committee on 2026-05-01.

## 2. Testing on a periodic basis (A1.3-R1)

Continuity testing runs on a **defined calendar**, with the depth of each test matched to the magnitude of the
disruption it simulates. Not every scenario justifies a full failover each quarter; every scenario justifies
*some* test each year.

| Test | Frequency | Depth | Verifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Backup restore of the priority data classes | **Every cycle (quarterly)** | Full restore to an isolated target, integrity verified | Recover |
| Worker-loss and lock-expiry test | Quarterly | Live fault injection in a non-production environment | Respond, resume |
| Availability-zone loss | **Semi-annually** | Fault injection removing a zone's capacity | Respond, recover, resume |
| Automatic-mitigation fault injection (each mechanism in the backup standard §4.2) | Semi-annually | Each mechanism individually triggered and timed | Respond |
| Model / inference-quota exhaustion and fallback | Semi-annually | Forced fallback to alternate model or region, degraded duration measured | Recover, resume |
| **Region loss — full failover exercise** | **Annually** | Rebuild from infrastructure-as-code in the alternate region, restore data, resume assessments | Respond, recover, resume |
| **Key-personnel-unavailable exercise** | **Annually** | Tabletop plus live execution of runbooks by the alternate only | Respond, recover |
| **Vendor / third-party unavailability** | **Annually**, per critical vendor class | Tabletop with live verification of the documented workaround where one exists | Respond, resume |
| Full continuity plan walkthrough with all role holders | Annually | Tabletop | All three |
| Communication-path test (customer, regulator, vendor contact chains) | Annually | Live contact verification | Respond |

Each test produces a written result: the scenario, the date, who participated, what was executed rather than
discussed, the measured times, what failed, and the revisions raised. **Response, recovery, and resumption are
assessed separately** — a test in which the team responded correctly but could not resume within the objective is
recorded as a failure of resumption, not as a qualified pass.

## 3. Scenario development from threat likelihood and magnitude (A1.3-R2)

Scenarios are **derived from the risk register**, not from a generic template. Each candidate threat is scored for
likelihood and magnitude, and the score determines both whether it is tested and how deeply:

| Scenario | Likelihood | Magnitude | Test depth chosen | Why this depth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loss of a worker process mid-assessment | **High** — expected in normal operation | Low: one assessment delayed | Quarterly live injection | Frequent enough that automatic handling must be continuously proven |
| Provider service degradation in one zone | **Medium** | Medium: reduced capacity | Semi-annual live injection | Plausible and partially automatic; needs measurement, not discussion |
| Inference quota exhaustion or model unavailability | **Medium** — the hardest constraint in the system | High: assessments cannot complete at all | Semi-annual live fallback | This is the single most likely cause of a total functional outage |
| Corruption or accidental deletion of evidence or results | **Medium** | **High**: potentially unrecoverable customer data | Quarterly restore test | Highest-consequence data risk; object versioning was enabled on all buckets 2026-08-18, so an in-place overwrite/delete is now directly recoverable, but the quarterly cadence is retained as prudent |
| Loss of the primary region | **Low** | **Very high**: total outage, with a data residual — backups are region-scoped and no cross-region copy is configured (§208, backup standard §6.5) | Annual full failover | Low likelihood but existential magnitude; infrastructure rebuilt from IaC, data recovery for region loss is a registered open item |
| Unavailability of a critical vendor | Low–Medium, varies by vendor | Medium–High | Annual per vendor class | Outside the entity's control, so the workaround is the control |
| Unavailability of key personnel | **Medium** — illness, departure, leave | Medium: recovery delayed, not prevented | Annual, executed by alternates only | The most commonly untested failure and the easiest to be wrong about |
| Compromise requiring deliberate shutdown | Low | High | Covered in the incident program's exercise set | Security-driven containment, tested there to avoid two competing processes |
| Simultaneous security and availability event | Low | Very high | Biennial combined tabletop | Rare, but reveals conflicts between containment and recovery priorities |

**Magnitude is measured against the availability commitment**, not against internal inconvenience. The scoring is
reviewed annually with the risk assessment, and a change in either dimension changes the test calendar — for
example, the evidence-corruption scenario moved from semi-annual to quarterly while object versioning was still
disabled; versioning was enabled on all buckets on 2026-08-18, and the quarterly cadence is retained as prudent
for the highest-consequence data risk.

## 4. Components from across the entity and its vendors (A1.3-R3)

Testing addresses **every component class relevant to an availability impairment**, including those the entity does
not operate. A test set that covered only the entity's own code would miss most of the real failure surface.

| Component | Owner | Relevance to availability | How testing covers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assessment agent and graph nodes | Entity | Total functional dependency | Fault injection; degraded-mode runs |
| Worker processes and the assessment lock | Entity | Concurrency and progress | Quarterly worker-loss test incl. lock expiry |
| Express server / runtime container | Entity | Entry point for every invocation | Restart and health-check tests |
| Infrastructure definitions | Entity | The rebuild path itself | Annual region failover rebuilds from them |
| Object storage (evidence, results, logs) | Provider | Data availability and durability | Restore tests; zone-loss injection |
| Record store (assessment state) | Provider | Progress and results | Point-in-time restore tests |
| **Model inference service** | **Provider** | **No inference, no assessment** | Semi-annual forced fallback |
| Key management | Provider | Encrypted data is unreadable without it | Key-availability verification during restore tests |
| Identity provider / federation | Provider | Workforce cannot access to recover | Covered in the key-personnel and region tests; break-glass path exercised |
| Cloud control plane | Provider | Cannot rebuild without it | Annual failover assumes it available; loss is a recorded, accepted risk |
| Frontend / scheduler service that invokes the agent | Entity (adjacent) | Assessments are not triggered | Interface contract tests; queue-drain behaviour |
| Assessed customer accounts | Customer | Scans fail or throttle | Pacing and backoff tests; partial-scan handling |
| Source control and build pipeline | Vendor | Cannot ship a fix during an incident | Annual vendor-unavailability scenario |
| Observability backend | Vendor | Recovering blind | Vendor-unavailability scenario; local logging fallback verified |
| Ticketing / task system | Vendor | Coordination record lost | Vendor-unavailability scenario; documented manual fallback |
| Communication platform | Vendor | Cannot coordinate or notify | Annual communication-path test with an out-of-band channel |

**Vendor components are tested as vendor components** — the entity does not simulate the vendor's internals; it
tests *its own response* to that vendor being unavailable, which is the only part it controls. Where the tested
workaround is manual, the test verifies a human can actually execute it under time pressure, not merely that it is
written down.

## 5. Lack of availability of key personnel or vendors (A1.3-R4)

This is the scenario most often assumed away, so it is specified in detail.

### 5.1 Key personnel

Every role in the incident response program has a **named primary and a named alternate**, and the annual exercise
is run **with every primary declared unavailable**. The alternates execute the runbooks unaided; the primaries may
not answer questions during the exercise, because a plan that works only while the expert is reachable has not been
tested.

| Dependency on a person | Failure it would cause | Mitigation tested |
|---|---|---|
| Incident commander unavailable | No one owns the disruption | Alternate commander runs the exercise end to end |
| Only one engineer knows the failover procedure | Region recovery stalls | Runbooks documented; exercise executed by an engineer who did not write them |
| Break-glass credential holder unavailable | Cannot perform recovery actions needing elevation | Escrow with a documented two-person retrieval path; retrieval is exercised, not assumed |
| Approver for emergency change unavailable | Fix cannot be authorised | Documented delegation chain; exercised in the tabletop |
| Sole holder of a vendor relationship or account | Cannot raise a vendor escalation | Vendor contacts and account details held in a shared, access-controlled record; retrieval exercised |
| Whole-team unavailability (severe illness, travel event) | No response at all | Escalation to the external retainers in the incident program's external-resource table; contact paths verified annually |

Findings from these exercises have repeatedly been the most valuable in this plan's history, because they surface
undocumented knowledge that no infrastructure test can reveal. **Single points of human dependency discovered in an
exercise are logged as deficiencies with owners and due dates**, exactly as technical findings are.

### 5.2 Vendors

For each critical vendor class the exercise establishes: what breaks immediately, what breaks after a delay, the
documented workaround, who is authorised to invoke it, and how long the entity can operate without the vendor
before the availability commitment is at risk. That last figure — **tolerable outage duration per vendor** — is
recorded, and where it is shorter than the vendor's own committed recovery time, the gap is a registered risk with
a named owner rather than an implicit hope.

Where no workaround exists — the cloud provider's control plane is the clear case — the plan says so explicitly
and records the dependency as an accepted risk with the reasoning, rather than inventing a mitigation that would
not be executed.

## 6. Testing the integrity and completeness of backup information (A1.3-R6)

Restore testing verifies **both** properties the criterion names, because they fail differently:

- **Integrity** — the restored data is *correct*: checksums match, records are internally consistent, encrypted
  objects decrypt with the keys actually available at restore time, and a sample of restored evidence is compared
  byte-for-byte against its recorded checksum. A restore that produces readable but altered data is a failure.
- **Completeness** — the restored data is *all there*: record counts and byte totals are reconciled against the
  source catalogue for the backup point, and the set of restored stores is reconciled against the live inventory of
  stores. **The most likely completeness failure is a data store that was never in the backup set**, so the
  reconciliation runs against the inventory rather than against the backup catalogue alone. A backup that restores
  perfectly and omits a whole store is a failure of completeness even though every job reported success.

Restore tests are performed **to an isolated target**, never over live data, and the test record states the backup
point used, the elapsed restore time against the recovery objective, the integrity result, the completeness
reconciliation, and any deviation. Priority classes — customer evidence, assessment results, the journal — are
tested **every cycle**; remaining classes rotate so each is covered at least annually. The rotation, and therefore
which classes are currently unverified in the present cycle, is stated in each test record rather than left
implicit; this is the subset limitation registered as an open deficiency in the backup standard §6.5.

## 7. Revising plans and systems based on results (A1.3-R5)

Test results **change things**, and the change is traceable in both directions.

1. Every finding is logged with a severity, an owner, and a due date.
2. Findings that require a system change enter the **change management process** with the test result cited as the
   trigger; findings that require a plan change are revisions to this document or to a runbook.
3. **Recovery objectives are revised to the measured reality** where a test shows the stated objective is not
   achievable — the objective changes, or the system changes, but the discrepancy is never left standing. A stated
   objective that a test has contradicted is the specific failure this rule prevents.
4. Findings are reviewed at the quarterly capacity and continuity review and reported to the Audit Committee.
5. **Reconciliation in both directions** is the review's first agenda item: every open finding has a change record
   or a documented risk acceptance, and every continuity-driven change record cites the finding that produced it.
6. A finding closed **without** change carries a written justification.

### 7.1 Results of the most recent cycle, and what they changed

Recorded here because a plan that describes a revision process without showing a revision is not evidence of one.

| Test | Result | Revision it forced |
|---|---|---|
| Priority-class restore test | **Pass.** Evidence, results and journal restored; integrity verified; completeness reconciled | Sample size for byte-level evidence comparison increased after the reconciliation took longer than expected |
| Worker-loss and lock-expiry | **Pass.** Lock expired and the assessment became re-runnable without operator action | None; recorded as no change warranted |
| Availability-zone loss | **Pass with finding.** Capacity recovered, but surviving-capacity throughput was below the figure the forecast assumed | Capacity forecast re-based on the *measured* surviving figure; the assumed figure was labelled an assumption until re-measured |
| Model fallback | **Pass with finding.** Fallback worked; degraded-mode assessment duration was materially longer than the primary path | Degraded duration recorded as a distinct capacity figure; alternate-region quota pre-arrangement raised as a standing capacity item |
| Region-loss failover | **Partial.** Service rebuilt from infrastructure-as-code within objective; **data restore could not be completed in the alternate region** because backups are region-scoped | Cross-region backup copy registered as an open deficiency (backup standard §6.5, due 2026-09-30); the region-loss recovery objective is currently labelled **unverified for data** rather than reported as met |
| Key-personnel-unavailable | **Pass with findings.** Alternates executed recovery; two runbook steps proved to rely on undocumented knowledge | Both runbooks amended; a documentation-review step added to the change policy's currency table |
| Vendor unavailability | **Pass with finding.** Observability-backend loss left the team recovering with reduced visibility | Local logging fallback documented and verified; tolerable outage duration recorded per vendor |
| Communication-path test | **Pass.** All contact chains reachable | Alternate security contact for the account remains unset — carried as an open item |

The region-loss row is the reason this plan states an unverified objective as unverified. Reporting that objective
as met would have been the single largest misstatement available in this document, and the test is what prevents it.
